Spindle and Feed Programming

Spindle speed (S) and feed rate (F) are the two cut-rate commands. Getting them right is the difference between a good surface finish and a broken tool. This page covers S, M03/M04/M05, feed modes G94/G95, G96 constant surface speed, and how to choose values from tooling data.

Concept

Two numbers control how fast the cutting happens: S (spindle speed) and F (feed rate). S tells the spindle how fast to turn; F tells the tool how fast to move through the material. On a mill, S is nearly always fixed. On a lathe, S can be fixed RPM or constant surface speed (G96), where the controller automatically changes RPM as the tool moves across changing diameters. Feed can be per minute (G94) or per revolution (G95). These are the two cut-rate words, and they are modal once set.

Why It Matters

Set S too high and the tool overheats, wears fast, or chatters. Set S too low and you waste time and rub the tool. Set F too high and the tool breaks or deflects; too low and you rub and work-harden the part. On a lathe, cutting at fixed RPM while facing from the outside diameter to the center means the surface speed drops to near zero at the center — that is why G96 exists. Getting these two words right is not optional: a broken tool or a scrapped batch usually traces back to a wrong S or F.

How It Works

Spindle Control: S, M03, M04, M05

S sets the spindle speed in revolutions per minute (RPM). M03 starts the spindle clockwise (forward), M04 counterclockwise (reverse), and M05 stops it. Most milling and turning tools cut clockwise when viewed from the spindle side. Drill, end mill, and face mill all use M03. You rarely use M04 except for specific tooling. Typical sequence: S2000 M03 on one line starts the spindle at 2000 RPM forward. The spindle must be started before the tool engages the material. Coolant (M08) typically starts after the spindle is at speed.

Feed Modes: G94 vs G95

The F command means different things depending on the active feed mode:

G94Feed per minutemm/minMills
G95Feed per revolutionmm/revLathes, threading
G99 (Haas lathe)Feed per revolutionmm/revHaas lathes

Why the difference matters: On a mill, the tool spins and the table feeds. F300 means 300 mm per minute. On a lathe, the part spins and the tool feeds. F0.2 means 0.2 mm per revolution. At 1000 RPM, that is 200 mm/min. If you accidentally program F0.2 on a mill in G94 mode, the tool feeds 0.2 mm/min — almost stopped. If you program F200 on a lathe in G95 mode, the tool feeds 200 mm per revolution — it crashes. Always know which feed mode is active.

How to Choose Speed and Feed

Speed and feed are not guesses. They come from the tool manufacturer's catalog: cutting speed (Vc in m/min) and feed per tooth (fz in mm/tooth). Convert them:

  • RPM = Vc × 1000 / (π × D) where D is tool diameter in mm.
  • Feed rate = RPM × fz × number of teeth (Z).

For a Ø10 carbide end mill cutting aluminum at Vc = 200 m/min, 2 teeth, fz = 0.05 mm/tooth: RPM = 200×1000/(3.14×10) = 6366 RPM, round to 6000. Feed = 6000×0.05×2 = 600 mm/min. Use the Cutting Speed Calculator and Feed Rate Calculator for these conversions. These are classroom assumptions; real values come from the tooling catalog and the machine's power curve.

Constant Surface Speed (G96) on Lathes

On a lathe, cutting at fixed RPM (G97) means the surface speed changes as the tool moves across different diameters: facing from Ø50 to center at S1000 starts at 157 m/min but drops to near 0 at center. G96 (constant surface speed) keeps the cutting speed constant: the spindle automatically slows down as the tool approaches center. Always pair G96 with a maximum RPM limit (G50 S2000) to prevent the spindle from overspeeding on small diameters. Threading always uses G97 fixed RPM.

Chip Load and Why It Matters

Feed per tooth (fz) is the chip load: how thick each flute cuts on one revolution. Too light and the tool rubs instead of cutting, generating heat and work-hardening. Too heavy and the edge chips or breaks. Chip load is set per material and per tool: a carbide end mill in aluminum may take 0.05–0.15 mm/tooth; in steel, 0.02–0.05 mm/tooth. The catalog gives the range; start at the middle and adjust for finish and tool life.

Feed rate = RPM × fz × number of flutes. A 4-flute end mill at 3000 RPM and fz 0.05 gives 600 mm/min. If the machine cannot hold that feed under load, reduce it rather than pushing the machine. Also note that radial engagement (ae) and axial engagement (ap) affect chip thinning: at light radial engagement, the chip is thinner than fz, and you may need to increase feed to keep a reasonable chip thickness.

Power and Torque Limits

Even if the math says 6000 RPM at 600 mm/min, the machine may not have enough spindle power. MRR (material removal rate) = width × depth × feed. If MRR exceeds what the spindle can drive, the spindle bogs down and the tool rubs. A small 7.5 kW mill cannot take a full 10 mm depth on a 20 mm carbide end mill in steel at 600 mm/min. Start conservatively: take a light cut, listen to the spindle, watch the load meter, and increase feed or depth until the load is reasonable. The tool catalog gives the maximum MRR for the tool; the machine's power curve gives the upper limit.

Surface Speed vs Spindle Speed

Cutting speed (Vc, m/min) is the speed of the tool edge relative to the workpiece surface. Spindle speed (RPM) is how fast the spindle turns. They are related by diameter: Vc = π × D × RPM / 1000. For a fixed Vc, a smaller tool needs higher RPM. This is why a Ø3 drill runs at much higher RPM than a Ø20 face mill for the same material. On a lathe, Vc is constant under G96 because the part diameter changes; on a mill, the tool diameter is fixed, so RPM is fixed under G97.

Spindle Direction: M03 vs M04

Almost all milling and turning tools cut in M03 (clockwise when viewed from the spindle side). Drills, end mills, face mills, and inserts all expect M03. M04 is counter-clockwise and is rarely used — it appears in special operations like left-handed tools or certain tapping cycles. A common mistake is running a standard end mill in M04: the tool cuts backwards, rubs instead of shears, and overheats quickly. If a program suddenly uses M04, check why before running it.

Feed in Threading

Threading on a lathe is different: the feed must exactly match the thread pitch. For M10×1.5, the feed is F1.5 mm/rev. The spindle must be in G97 fixed RPM (not G96), because the thread lead is fixed by the feed and any RPM change would cause the tool to chase the wrong helix. Threading also uses a dedicated cycle (G76 or G92) that controls the number of passes and infeed. Never use CSS for threading.

Spindle Warm-Up

On a cold machine, running at full RPM immediately can cause thermal growth in the spindle bearings. Many shops have a short warm-up program that ramps spindle speed from low to high over 10–15 minutes before the first production part. This stabilizes spindle temperature and improves accuracy. The warm-up program is not a cutting program; it just spins the spindle at increasing speeds with no tool in the cut.

Example: Milling and Turning

Milling (G94): G54 G90 S2000 M03 G00 X0 Y0 G43 H01 Z50. M08 G00 Z5. G01 Z-2. F100. G01 X50. F300. Spindle at 2000 RPM, plunge feed 100 mm/min, feed cut 300 mm/min.

Turning (G96/G95): G50 S2500 G96 S180 M03 G95 F0.2. Caps RPM at 2500, holds 180 m/min surface speed, feed 0.2 mm/rev. At Ø50 the RPM is about 1146; at Ø25 the controller raises it to about 2292, capped at 2500. The surface speed at the tool tip stays near 180 m/min across the cut.

Starting Conservatively

Tool catalog speeds are maximum recommended values under ideal conditions. In a real shop with a worn machine, less-than-perfect setup, and mixed materials, start at 70% of catalog speed and 80% of catalog feed. Listen to the spindle: a high-pitch whine or a chattering sound means the speed is too high or the setup is not rigid. A humming, steady sound with smooth chips is good. If chips are long and stringy, the feed may be too low; if chips are blue or burnt, the speed is too high or coolant is insufficient.

Feed Rate Override

Nearly all CNC machines have a feed rate override knob (0–150%) and a spindle speed override knob. These are for setup and debugging, not for production. During the first cut, set feed override to 50% and watch the cut; if it looks good, ramp to 100%. In production, run at 100% and let the catalog values do their job. Leaving the feed override at 50% on a production machine halves the cycle time and wastes money.

Spindle Speed Override and CSS

Under G97 fixed RPM, the spindle override knob scales the S command: S2000 at 80% override runs at 1600 RPM. Under G96 constant surface speed, the spindle override still works, but the controller adjusts RPM to maintain the surface speed — the override changes the target surface speed rather than directly scaling RPM. This is a subtle but important difference: on a lathe in CSS, the override knob effectively scales the cutting speed, not the spindle RPM directly.

Feeds and Speeds Are a Starting Point

The calculated RPM and feed are a starting point, not a command. The real answer depends on tool condition, workpiece material batch variation, machine rigidity, coolant pressure, and how deep and wide the cut is. Experienced machinists listen to the cut, look at the chips, and adjust. The program sets a reasonable starting point; the operator fine-tunes on the first piece.

Feed Hold and Single Block

The feed-hold button pauses the axis motion without stopping the spindle. Single-block mode executes one block at a time, so you can step through a new program line by line. These are setup tools: use them on the first part, not in production. When single-block is on, the machine pauses after each line, which is tedious for a long production run but essential for catching unexpected motion. Always use single block and 50% feed override on the first piece of a new program.

Common Mistakes

  • Using mm/min feed on a lathe defaulting to mm/rev — F200 on a G95 lathe means 200 mm per revolution, which is a crash. Check the active feed mode.
  • Starting spindle before the tool is clear of the part — start spindle while the tool is at safe Z, not at cut depth.
  • Fixed RPM for all diameters on a lathe — use G96 CSS for facing and OD turning; G97 only for threading.
  • Running at catalog speed in poor conditions — catalog speeds assume sharp tool, rigid setup, good coolant. Start conservatively.
  • Forgetting G50 before G96 — without an RPM cap, facing near center commands excessive speed.
  • Setting S without M03 — S2000 alone does not start the spindle; M03 is required to rotate.

Practice

1. On a Haas lathe in G99, what does F0.2 mean?

Show answer

0.2 mm per revolution, not 0.2 mm/min. At S1000, actual table feed = 1000 × 0.2 = 200 mm/min.

2. Why does a lathe use G96 (CSS) for facing?

Show answer

As the tool moves across the face, the cutting diameter changes. Constant surface speed keeps the chip load and tool wear consistent across the entire cut.

3. Ø10 end mill, 2 teeth, Vc=100 m/min, fz=0.05. What RPM and feed?

Show answer

RPM = 100×1000/(3.14×10) = 3183, use 3000. Feed = 3000×0.05×2 = 300 mm/min.

4. Why is G50 S2500 written before G96?

Show answer

It caps maximum RPM so facing to a small diameter does not command unsafe speed.

5. Why switch back to G97 before threading on a lathe?

Show answer

Threading requires a fixed RPM so each pass starts at the same spindle angle; CSS would change RPM mid-thread.
