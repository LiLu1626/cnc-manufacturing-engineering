Milling Strategies: Facing, Slots & Pockets

Different milling features call for different strategies. This page covers face milling, slot milling, pocket milling, climb vs conventional cutting, stepover, and how to choose the right approach for each feature.

Concept

Milling is not just moving the tool in a line. The way the tool engages the material — how wide, how deep, which direction — determines tool life, surface finish, and cycle time. Face milling removes material from the top surface. Slot milling cuts a channel with full-width tool engagement. Pocket milling clears an enclosed area, typically with raster or zig-zag passes. Each strategy uses different stepover, axial depth, and toolpath direction.

Why It Matters

Using a Ø10 end mill to slot through a 12 mm deep block in one pass overloads the spindle and snaps the tool. Pocketing without climb milling causes chatter and poor surface finish. Taking stepover too wide exceeds spindle power. Strategy choice directly affects tool life, surface finish, and whether you can actually make the part on your machine.

How It Works

Stepover (Radial Engagement)

Stepover is the width of each pass relative to the cutter diameter. For a Ø10 tool at 50% stepover, each pass removes 5 mm width.

OperationStepover (% of cutter D)Ø10 Tool Width/Pass
Roughing50–75%5–7.5 mm
Semi-finishing30–50%3–5 mm
Finishing10–25%1–2.5 mm

Wider stepover = fewer passes = faster cycle time, but heavier load on the tool and spindle. Narrower stepover = lighter cut = better finish but more passes.

Climb vs Conventional Milling

This is one of the most important strategy decisions:

  • Climb milling (down-milling): the cutter rotates in the same direction as the table feed. The chip starts thick and gets thinner. The tool cuts cleanly, pushes the work down, and produces a better surface finish. Preferred on CNC machines, which have preloaded ball screws with no backlash.
  • Conventional milling (up-milling): the cutter rotates against the feed direction. The chip starts thin (rubbing) and gets thicker. The tool rubs and work-hardens the surface, and tends to climb the workpiece. Traditional manual machines used conventional because of backlash; CNC machines prefer climb.

Axial Depth of Cut (ap)

Axial depth is how deep the tool cuts per pass, in the Z direction. For a Ø10 end mill: roughing at 1×D (10 mm axial) is common in aluminum; in steel, 0.5×D (5 mm) is more typical. Full-width slotting (100% radial engagement) requires much smaller axial depth — often 0.2–0.5×D — because the tool is fully buried in chips.

Example

Face milling example: Face mill a 100 mm wide steel plate with a Ø50 face mill. Stepover = 35 mm (70% of Ø50). Number of passes = ceil(100/35) = 3 passes. At feed F300 mm/min, each pass traverses 100 mm. Facing time ≈ 3 × 100/300 = 100 seconds total (including approach/retract, about 2 minutes).

Pocketing example: Mill a 40×40 mm pocket with a Ø10 end mill at 50% stepover. The pocket width is 40 mm; each pass removes 5 mm width. Number of raster passes = 40/5 = 8 passes. If the pocket is 10 mm deep, you might rough in 2 axial passes (5 mm each), then finish the walls and floor separately.

Choosing a Strategy Is a Compromise

No single milling strategy is "best." Face milling is fast for large flats but cannot reach deep pockets. Slotting cuts full-width slots but overloads the tool. Pocketing requires a ramp or plunge. Climb milling gives a better finish but needs a rigid, preloaded ball-screw machine. Conventional milling is safer on old machines with backlash but work-hardens the surface. The right choice depends on the part geometry, the machine, the tool, and the tolerance — not on a dogma.

Radial engagement (ae, the width of cut as a fraction of tool diameter) is the number most beginners ignore. A full-width slot (ae = 1.0) loads both flutes equally and runs hot; a 10% stepover (ae = 0.1) lets the tool cool between cuts and uses higher rpm. For aluminum, ae around 0.3–0.5 with high rpm and moderate feed is a good starting point; for steel, ae closer to 0.1–0.2 keeps chip load and heat under control. Stepover 5 mm on a Ø10 cutter (ae = 0.5) leaves 50% overlap between rows — not 8 rows, but 7, as the row positions run from one edge to the other.

Plunge milling (axial feed straight down) is for deep pockets where a ramp angle is too shallow. A center-cutting end mill can plunge, but the center teeth have poor chip evacuation — use peck plunges in deep material. Trochoidal milling — a circular toolpath that keeps ae small while Z depth is large — is the modern high-efficiency strategy for hard materials, but it requires a rigid machine and a chipbreaker insert. Start simple: face, then slot, then pocket. Add trochoidal only when you need it.

After choosing the strategy, verify it on paper: does the toolholder clear the vise? Does the spindle reach the rpm at the chosen cutting speed? Does the feed stay within the machine's feed-hold? A strategy that looks perfect on the drawing but exceeds spindle power will stall on the machine — that is a setup problem, not a strategy problem.

Stepover Math: Rows vs Passes

For a 40 mm wide cut with a Ø10 tool (radius 5) and 5 mm stepover, the tool center runs from one wall (center at X=5) to the other wall (center at X=35). Stepover 5 gives center positions X=5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35 — that is 7 rows, not 8. The first row leaves half a radius against the wall (no overlap), and the last row likewise. Beginners often count 40/5 = 8 rows and overshoot the last row by half a step.

For a slot (full width = tool diameter), there is no stepover — the tool cuts its own width. For a pocket, the stepover is the raster pitch between adjacent rows. For a face mill, the stepover is the width of cut as a fraction of the cutter diameter (ae = 0.5 means half the cutter overlaps the previous pass).

The 7-row example above covers the 40 mm width with 5 mm stepover. Add a wall-cleanup pass at the end: one final pass along each pocket wall to clean up the scallop left by the raster. This is why real pocket programs have a raster section followed by a wall section — the raster leaves a scallop, and the wall pass removes it.

Face Milling a Large Flat

Face milling uses a large-diameter cutter with multiple inserts (often 40–100 mm) to take a shallow cut (0.5–2 mm) across a flat surface. The cutter is usually 1.2–1.5 times the width of the cut, so one pass covers the full width. Face milling is fast but requires a rigid machine and a stable workholding — a flimsy setup chatters badly under a heavy face mill.

The lead-in and lead-out for a face mill should be straight, not angled. Start the cutter off the edge of the part, feed into the cut, and exit past the far edge. Do not start the cutter spinning over the part — it leaves a witness mark. Climb or conventional on a face mill: climb is preferred on CNC machines, but the insert corner radius and the feed direction interact to leave a wave. If the finish is bad, check the insert condition first, then the feed, then the speed.

Slot milling with an end mill cuts full width (ae = 1.0). This loads both flutes equally and is the heaviest cut an end mill takes. Keep slotting depth under 2× diameter on a flat end mill; deeper slots use a center-cutting end mill with pecking. Slotting also generates full-width chips that do not evacuate easily — use flood coolant and reduce feed if chips pack.

Trochodial and High-Efficiency Milling

Trochoidal milling cuts a circular path with a small radial engagement (ae = 0.1–0.3 diameter) while taking a full axial depth. It keeps chip load constant and reduces heat, which extends tool life in hard materials. It is not faster for aluminum — it is for hardened steel, titanium, and deep slots where a full-width slot would overload the spindle. If your machine has the spindle power for a full-width slot, trochoidal is unnecessary complexity.

High-efficiency milling (HEM) is the CAM-driven version of the same idea: constant chip load, small ae, large ap. It requires a solid carbide end mill with multiple flutes and a machine that can move at the programmed feed. On a small Haas VF-2, HEM may not run faster than conventional roughing; on a 12,000 rpm machining center, it can double metal removal rate. Match the strategy to the machine, not to the marketing brochure.

The beginner default: climb mill, 0.5–0.7 stepover, 1–1.5× diameter axial depth, moderate feed. Add trochoidal only when you have a specific problem (chatter, tool life, deep slot) that the default cannot solve.

Common Mistakes

  • Full-width slotting in one deep pass — the tool is 100% engaged and cannot evacuate chips. Use smaller axial depth or peck drilling for deep slots.
  • Conventional milling on a CNC machine — CNC machines have no backlash; climb milling gives better finish and longer tool life. Program climb.
  • Stepover too wide — exceeds spindle power and torque. Start conservative and increase as you learn the machine.
  • No air gap in pocket corners — if the tool is Ø10 and the pocket corner is Ø6, the tool cannot reach the corner. Program a corner cleanup pass or use a smaller tool.
  • Plunge feeding too fast — end mills are not drills. Plunge at 1/3 to 1/2 of the normal feed, or use a center-cutting end mill.

Practice

1. Ø12 end mill at 50% stepover. Width per pass?

Ans

12 × 0.5 = 6 mm per pass.

2. Climb or conventional on a CNC machining center?

Ans

Climb. CNC ball screws are preloaded with no backlash, so climb milling is safe and preferred for finish and tool life.

3. Pocket 40×40 mm, Ø10 tool at 50% stepover. How many raster passes?

Ans

Effective cut width = 10 × 0.5 = 5 mm. 40/5 = 8 passes across the pocket.

4. Why is full-width slotting limited to shallow axial depth?

Ans

At 100% radial engagement, chips cannot evacuate. The tool re-cut chips, overheats, and snaps. Use small axial depth (0.2–0.5×D) or peck cycles.
