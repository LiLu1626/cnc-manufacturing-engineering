Tool Management & Monitoring

A shop that loses track of which tool has how many parts on it wastes time and scrap. Tool identification, offset records and simple life tracking are the basics; monitoring catches wear before it ruins a part.

Level: BPrerequisite: 28 Tool Life TestingScope: shop practice
After this lesson, you can:
  • Identify tools and record offsets correctly
  • Track tool life by parts or time
  • Describe simple in-process wear monitoring

Concept

Tool management means knowing which tool is where, its offset values, and how many parts it has cut. Life tracking counts parts or time per edge so the tool is replaced before it fails. Monitoring — from operator observation to load/acoustic sensors — flags wear or breakage during the run.

Why It Matters

Without tracking, a tool runs until it scrapes a part or breaks in the cut. With simple records, you replace the edge on schedule, reuse known offsets, and reorder before stock runs out. Management turns tooling from a fire-fight into a controlled process.

How It Works

Label each tool assembly, record its length/diameter offset, and log parts per edge. When the count reaches the proven life limit, index or replace. Light monitoring (spindle load rising, finish changing) gives early warning between scheduled changes.

Worked Example

A recurring job uses T03; the proven life is 120 parts. The operator logs each run and indexes T03 at 120 parts — before wear hurts the bore. Offsets are recorded so a replacement tool touches in once and repeats. No surprise scrapped bores.

What a record should contain

At minimum: tool assembly ID, holder and tool number, measured length/diameter offset, the job it runs on, parts-per-edge at replacement, and the reason it was changed (wear, chipping, or scheduled). Over time this data reveals patterns — which jobs wear tools fastest, which grade outlasts another, and when to reorder before stock runs out. Without it, every setup starts from zero memory and offsets drift.

Monitoring levels

The simplest monitoring is the operator noticing a rising spindle load, a dull tone, or a worsening finish between parts. More advanced shops track spindle power/current against a baseline; a rising draw signals wear before the part suffers. Even a basic in-process check catches the slow drift that scheduled replacement alone might miss between two index points.

How counting parts pays off

Put numbers to the habit. If T03 has a proven life of 120 parts per edge and the shop runs 480 parts of this job a day, that is four edge changes a day — easy to lose track of without a log. When every operator indexes at 120 regardless of who runs the machine, bores stay on size and no one pushes an edge to 150 "because it still looks fine." The offset record means the replacement tool is loaded with known length and diameter values, so touch-in takes minutes, not a trial-and-error first part. Over weeks, the log also shows whether a batch of inserts consistently fails at 90 rather than 120 parts — a signal that the grade or the incoming material has changed, which triggers a look at pages 27–28 rather than an expensive surprise.

Reading the spindle-load signal

The cheapest monitoring sensor you already own is the spindle load meter. A fresh edge at the locked cut draws a known current; as flank wear grows, the edge rubs more and the same cut needs more power, so load climbs before the part visibly suffers. A simple workflow establishes the baseline on the first good part, then watches for a rise — say 15–20% above baseline — as the trigger to inspect the edge, between scheduled 120-part changes. This catches the slow drift that a part counter alone misses: if T03 suddenly starts drawing higher load at part 80, the edge may be chipping or the incoming material harder, and you learn that at part 80 instead of discovering a bad bore at part 119. Load is not a precision instrument, but as a free trip-wire it is far better than waiting for the operator to notice a dull tone. Combine it with the parts counter and the offset log and you have a closed loop: schedule changes at proven life, and investigate any edge that departs from the baseline early.

Common Mistakes

  • Running a tool past its proven life. It scrapes. Fix: count parts and change on schedule.
  • Losing offset records. Setup time grows. Fix: log tool offsets with the assembly.

Practice

Q1. Why count parts per edge?

To replace the tool on schedule before wear or breakage ruins parts.
