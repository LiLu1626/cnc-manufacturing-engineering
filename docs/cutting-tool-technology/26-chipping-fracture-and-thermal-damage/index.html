Chipping, Fracture & Thermal Damage

Not all tool death is gradual wear. A chipped edge, a sudden fracture, or heat cracks the edge in one hit. These are mechanical or thermal overloads — and the fix is different from slowing down for wear.

Level: KPrerequisite: 25 Wear MechanismsScope: classroom overview
After this lesson, you can:
  • Distinguish chipping, fracture and thermal cracking
  • Identify overload vs interruption vs thermal-shock causes
  • Pick a corrective direction for each

Concept

Chipping is small fragments breaking off the edge — the edge looks nicked. Fracture / breakage is a large, sudden failure of the insert or tool body. Thermal cracks are heat-cycle cracks across the rake/flank, common when coolant floods a hot edge. These are catastrophic or sub-catastrophic, not the smooth wear land of page 25.

Why It Matters

Catastrophic failure ruins the part and can damage the holder. It usually signals an overload: feed too high, an interruption the grade cannot take, a brittle grade in a shock cut, or thermal shock. The fix is not "run slower for wear" but "remove the shock / choose a tougher edge / control coolant."

Worked Example

An insert in a steel facing cut with scale on entry chips at each pass — the brittle finishing grade is taking repeated impacts. Move to a tougher grade, reduce entry shock, or add edge preparation. If instead the insert has a checker pattern of heat cracks, flooding a hot edge is thermal shock — switch to dry/air or through-tool coolant the maker specifies.

Telling fracture from gradual wear

Gradual wear produces a smooth, predictable land that grows over many parts. Chipping and fracture produce a sudden, irregular notch or a missing piece of edge — and they usually appear within the first few parts, not after a long life. Thermal damage shows as a discolored, heat-tinted edge, often with a checked or crazed surface, from running too hot (excessive speed or failed coolant). The first question after an edge fails is therefore: did it wear in over time, or did it break suddenly? A sudden break points at shock, an edge that is too sharp, or an overloaded cut; heat tint points at speed or cooling; a growing land is normal flank wear. Matching the response to the symptom (page 27) avoids the classic mistake of slowing a chipped tool when the real problem was rigidity or brittleness.

Worked distinction: heat crack vs edge chip

Two inserts come back from the same job and both look "damaged." The first shows a regular, cross-hatched network of fine cracks across the rake face, and the edge has a straw or blue tint — that is thermal fatigue: the hot edge was cycled by intermittent coolant or interrupted engagement. The corrective direction is not a tougher grade but a thermal one: run dry/air where the maker requires, or switch to through-tool coolant applied consistently rather than flooding on and off. The second insert has an irregular missing chunk on the corner and no heat tint — that is mechanical overload, an impact in an interrupted cut on too brittle a geometry. Here the fix is a tougher grade, honed edge prep, or reduced engagement. Reading the pattern — regular checked cracks with colour versus a single impact notch — tells you whether to change the heat strategy or the edge toughness, and prevents the classic mistake of buying a tougher grade for a thermally cracked edge.

Why edge preparation fixes chipping

The reason a hone (page 06) helps a chipping edge is mechanical: a brand-new sharp corner is a stress concentrator that takes the full impact of every interruption at one tiny point. A light hone or T-land rounds that corner just enough to spread the contact over a small chamfer, so the shock is distributed instead of being concentrated on the theoretical edge. This is why the worked example's response to a chipping finishing grade is not only "switch grade" but "add edge prep": a sharp finishing edge in an interrupted cut is structurally overloaded regardless of how hard the grade is. The trade-off is that honing costs a little sharpness, so a gummy material that wants a razor edge (page 05) may built-up-edge after an over-honed edge — the preparation is matched to whether the cut is interrupted (needs toughness) or finishing (needs sharpness). When chipping persists even after a tougher grade and a hone, the remaining suspect is not the edge at all but the setup: chatter (page 22) and overhang (page 21) deliver the same repeated impact that brittleness does, and must be removed before more edge geometry is tried.

Common Mistakes

  • Responding to chipping by just slowing speed. The cause is shock/brittleness. Fix: tougher grade, edge prep, reduce interruption.
  • Ignoring chatter as a cause. Vibration chips the edge. Fix: rigidity first (pages 21–22).
  • Flooding a thermal-shock-prone edge. Cracks it. Fix: follow coolant guidance.

Practice

Q1. Small nicks on the edge from interruption — what?

Chipping; use a tougher grade or edge prep.

Q2. Heat-cycle cracks across the edge — cause?

Thermal shock from flooding a hot edge.
