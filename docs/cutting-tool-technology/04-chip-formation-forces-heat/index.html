Chip Formation, Forces & Heat

Every cut is controlled plastic deformation: the edge shears a layer, the chip flows up the rake face, and 99% of the power becomes heat. Understanding chip shape, the three forces and where heat goes explains chatter, built-up edge, crater wear and tool breakage before you see them.

Level: KPrerequisite: 03 Cutting Data & UnitsScope: metric; classroom examples
After this lesson, you can:
  • Describe continuous, segmented and discontinuous chips and what each means
  • Identify the three force directions and what each predicts
  • Explain how heat distributes and why speed concentrates it at the edge
  • Read chip shape as a diagnostic signal for feed and speed

Concept

Metal cutting is not “scraping”: the tool wedge forces the workpiece material to shear along a narrow shear zone, and the sheared layer flows up the rake face as a chip. The chip is thicker than the undeformed chip because it compresses in the shear zone. The way that chip forms falls into three main types:

  • Continuous chips — ductile materials at adequate speed; ideal but stringy, so they need a chipbreaker.
  • Segmented (serrated) chips — titanium, austenitic stainless, hardened steel; partly sheared then fractured, with high heat.
  • Discontinuous chips — brittle materials (cast iron) or very light cuts; chips break into small pieces naturally.

A built-up edge (BUE) occurs when workpiece material welds to the rake face at low cutting speed; it changes the actual geometry and ruins finish.

Why It Matters

The chip is the only part of the process you can see and measure without instruments. A long, snarled chip means the feed or chipbreaker window is wrong; a blue, hot chip means speed is high and heat is leaving through the chip; a shiny, welded edge means BUE. Forces tell you about rigidity and tool load: high radial force causes deflection and chatter; high tangential force drives power consumption. Heat distribution tells you whether coolant is reaching the right place. Reading these signs is how you catch a failing process before it ruins a part or snaps an insert.

How It Works

The three force components

Cutting forces and what they predict
ForceDirectionWhat it drives
Tangential (main) force FcOpposes rotation / cutting speedPower consumption, spindle load
Radial force FpPerpendicular to the surface, pushing tool awayDeflection, vibration, chatter
Axial / feed force FfAlong feed directionFeed mechanism load, chip thickness

For a given depth, radial force is the enemy of thin walls and long tools: it deflects the tool radially, causing taper and chatter. Positive rake geometry lowers cutting forces overall; negative rake is stronger but pushes harder.

Where the heat goes

Roughly, in conventional machining: most heat leaves in the chip, a smaller share goes into the tool, and a small fraction into the workpiece. At higher cutting speed, a larger fraction of heat travels in the chip — which sounds good, but the edge temperature still rises sharply with speed. This is why speed is the dominant lever on crater wear and edge softening, while feed mainly affects mechanical loading.

Chip thickness and the shear angle

The undeformed chip thickness depends on feed and engagement. In milling it is not constant: for a mild climb cut with fz and radial engagement, the chip is thin at entry and grows. Thinner chips rub instead of shear, which is why too-light feed work-hardens stainless and accelerates flank wear.

Worked Example

Use the locked milling baseline from page 03: n = 3800 RPM, zc = 4, fz = 0.04 mm, giving vf = 608 mm/min. If radial engagement leaves an average undeformed chip thickness near the 0.04 mm design value, the edge shears cleanly. If the operator, chasing a better finish, drops fz to 0.01 mm on stainless, the chip no longer shears — it rubs, work-hardens the surface, and flank wear rises even though “the cut looks lighter.” This is the classic too-light-feed failure.

Classroom assumption: force magnitudes and heat fractions are typical textbook ranges, not measured values for a specific setup. Always verify rigidity and load with your own cut.

Reading the chip as a free gauge

Because the chip is the only thing in the process you can see without a meter, treat it as a free diagnostic after every new setup. A short, controlled curl in the expected colour means the chipbreaker window and feed are right. A long, snarled, spring-like chip that wants to wind around the part means the chipformer is not breaking the material at this feed — raise feed slightly or select a different chipformer geometry rather than pushing speed. A blue or straw-coloured chip in steel is normal evidence that heat is leaving through the chip; a dull, dusty powder instead of a flow means you are rubbing a brittle material or taking an absurdly light cut. A shiny, welded smear on the rake face is built-up edge from running too slowly on ductile material. None of these requires an instrument — only the habit of looking at the chip before you walk away from the first part.

From tangential force to spindle power

The tangential force Fc is the one that spins the cut, so it sets the power draw. A useful rough relation is power P = Fc · vc, where Fc is the main cutting force and vc the cutting speed. If a turning cut needed Fc around 1000 N at vc = 120 m/min, then P = 1000 N × (120/60) m/s = 2000 W — about 2 kW at the tool, before accounting for drive and mechanical efficiency. The engineering point is not the exact number but the levers: power rises with the material's specific cutting force (harder, gummy materials need more Fc for the same chip section), with the chip cross-section (depth × feed), and directly with cutting speed. This is why a machine with limited spindle power forces you to reduce depth or feed, not speed, if you want to keep MRR — and why the radial force, though it does no work, is still the one you manage when thin walls or slender bars chatter. The forces and the heat they generate are therefore the bridge between a chosen cut and both the machine's power limit and the tool's life limit (pages 28–29).

Why built-up edge is a low-speed problem

Built-up edge (BUE) is the clearest sign that speed, not feed, is wrong. At very low cutting speed, the newly sheared workpiece material has enough time to weld onto the cold rake face before it flows away; that welded lump becomes the actual cutting edge, smearing the surface and breaking off in chunks that ruin finish and flank the edge. Raise speed and the contact time drops, the edge runs hotter, and the welded layer no longer sticks — BUE disappears. This is why a poor finish in a ductile material is often fixed by increasing vc rather than dropping it, the opposite of the intuition that "slower is safer". It also explains the temperature picture on this page: the same heat that looks harmful is what prevents welding. The diagnostic loop is therefore split by symptom — welded, shiny, erratic edge and a rough finish point to speed too low (BUE), while a fast crater on the rake face and a straw/blue hot edge point to speed too high. Feed controls chip thickness and mechanical load; speed controls edge temperature and chemical wear. They are different knobs, and the chip tells you which one to turn.

Why most heat leaves in the chip, not the coolant

It is natural to imagine the coolant is what keeps the edge cool, but the dominant heat transport in machining is physical: the chip itself carries the shear heat away. The work of shearing the layer becomes heat concentrated in the narrow shear zone, and because the chip immediately leaves the edge and flies off, that heat leaves with it. This is why a blue chip in steel is normal — it is the chip doing its job, carrying heat out of the cut. Coolant's real role is narrower: it keeps the edge and workpiece from soaking up the residual heat that does enter them, and it lubricates the rake face, but it cannot and should not be expected to "pull the heat out of the shear zone" the way a radiator would. The practical consequences are two. First, cranking flood volume does not rescue an edge that is running too fast — the heat is generated at the shear zone regardless, and the chip already carries most of it. Second, when the chip cannot leave (packed in a hole or pocket, page 23), the heat stays at the edge and the tool overheats even with coolant pouring in — which is why chip evacuation and heat control are the same problem. Speed raises edge temperature faster than coolant can compensate, which is why speed remains the lever that governs crater wear.

Common Mistakes

  • Reducing feed to get a better finish. Too-light feed rubs, work-hardens and worsens finish. Fix: keep feed in the designed chip-thickness window and use nose radius/geometry for finish.
  • Ignoring the chip as a diagnostic. A blue, curled chip is normal; a snarled, welded or dusty chip is information. Fix: observe chip shape after every setup.
  • Blaming the coating for chatter. Chatter is a rigidity/engagement problem (radial force), not a coating problem. Fix: reduce radial engagement, shorten overhang, change speed.
  • Assuming coolant removes most heat. Most heat leaves in the chip. Fix: direct coolant at the edge/chip interface where it matters, and use it consistently.

Practice

Q1. Name the three chip types and one material that gives each.

Continuous (ductile steel/aluminum), segmented (titanium, stainless, hardened steel), discontinuous (cast iron/brittle).

Q2. Which force most directly causes deflection and chatter?

The radial force Fp, pushing the tool away from the surface.

Q3. Why does dropping feed on stainless often increase wear?

A too-thin chip rubs rather than shears, work-hardening the surface and accelerating flank wear.

Q4. Where does most cutting heat leave?

In the chip; only a smaller share enters the tool and a small share the workpiece.
