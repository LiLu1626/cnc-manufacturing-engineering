Rake & Clearance Angles

The wedge at the cutting edge is defined by three angles: rake, clearance and wedge. Get them right and the edge cuts with low force; get them wrong and it rubs or breaks. This page explains positive versus negative rake, why clearance prevents rubbing, and how nose radius trades finish against strength.

Level: KPrerequisite: 04 Chip Formation, Forces & HeatScope: metric; classroom examples
After this lesson, you can:
  • Define rake, clearance and wedge and how they sum
  • Compare positive and negative rake and choose between them
  • Explain why too much clearance weakens the edge and too little causes rubbing
  • Select a nose radius from finish and rigidity requirements

Concept

A cutting edge is a wedge. The rake face is the surface the chip flows up; its angle relative to the normal to the surface is the rake angle (γ). The flank is the surface behind the edge that must not touch the machined surface; its gap angle is the clearance / relief angle (α). The remaining metal in the wedge is the wedge angle (β). In a 2-D section:

α + β + γ = 90°

Positive rake tilts the rake face so the chip flows easily and forces are low, but the edge is thinner. Negative rake presents a stronger, blunter edge that takes interrupted cuts and high feeds, but pushes harder and needs more power.

Why It Matters

Geometry is the cheapest lever you have — changing the rake designation on an insert often costs nothing extra yet changes force, heat and tool life more than many coating changes. A positive-rake insert in a heavy interrupted cut will crater or chip immediately; a negative-rake insert in a long, slender aluminum job will chatter and rub because it pushes too hard. Reading the geometry column of the catalog is the difference between choosing a tool that lasts and one that breaks on the first part.

Clearance angle

Clearance is small — typically a few degrees. Too little and the flank rubs the freshly machined surface, generating heat and a poor finish; too much and the wedge is so thin that the edge crumbles. The catalog's clearance designation is matched to the operation; you rarely change it by hand on indexable inserts.

Nose radius

The nose radius (rε) rounds the tip. A larger nose radius gives a better theoretical finish (see the Ra = f²/(32·rε) relation on page 03) but increases radial force and chatter risk on slender setups. This is the central trade-off: finish wants a big nose radius, rigidity wants a small one.

Worked Example

Two turning inserts finish the same shaft to the same feed f = 0.10 mm/rev. Insert A uses rε = 0.4 mm; Insert B uses rε = 0.8 mm. Using the locked roughness formula:

A: Ra = 0.10² / (32 × 0.4) = 0.000781 mm = 0.781 µm
B: Ra = 0.10² / (32 × 0.8) = 0.000391 mm = 0.391 µm

B predicts half the ideal roughness — but B also pushes a larger radial force. On a rigid, short-shaft setup B wins; on a long, slender bar supported only at one end, B can chatter and A is the safer choice. The geometry choice depends on the setup, not just the drawing.

Classroom assumption: the 0.781 / 0.391 µm values are ideal geometric predictions from the locked formula; measured finish will be worse and must be confirmed on the part.

Approach angle: the lever you can't ignore

Rake and nose radius get the attention, but the lead (approach) angle quietly sets both the radial force and the chip thickness. A large lead angle spreads the same feed over a longer cutting edge, which thins the chip and lets you run more feed — but it also increases the radial pushing force that deflects slender shafts. A small lead angle keeps the cut axial and gentler radially, which is why a 90° shoulder mill or a small-lead turning insert is preferred on thin walls and long bars. The practical consequence for the nose-radius choice is that they must be read together: on a rigid roughing setup you can afford both a large nose radius and a moderate lead angle; on a slender job you reduce nose radius and keep a lead angle that does not push radially, or chatter wins regardless of how sharp the edge is. This is why catalogs list the approach angle as part of the geometry designation rather than an afterthought.

Why negative rake needs a strong machine & a chipbreaker

The strength of a negative-rake edge does not come free: because the rake face tilts the other way, the chip is not sheared off a sharp knife but pushed up a near-flat face, which raises both the cutting force and the power drawn at the spindle. A negative-rake insert on a small, lightly-manned machine or a slender setup can chatter or deflect even though the edge itself is strong, and it tends to form a harder-to-control chip — which is why nearly all negative-rake inserts carry a pressed chipbreaker (page 06) built into the rake face. This is the real reason the geometry designation is read together with the machine: negative rake is for rigid, powerful roughing on a stout spindle; positive rake is for lighter, ductile or slender work where low force matters more than edge strength. The α+β+γ=90° sum shows why: make γ negative and the wedge β automatically grows, so the edge gets stronger but the face pushes harder — the angles trade against each other inside one fixed 90°, you cannot make the edge both razor-sharp and bulletproof at once.

Why clearance is only a few degrees

The clearance angle looks like a small, almost free parameter — a few degrees — but it is tightly constrained by the same 90° sum. Give the flank more clearance to be safe from rubbing and the wedge angle β shrinks, which thins and weakens the edge; give less clearance and the flank scrapes the freshly machined surface, generating heat, a poor finish and accelerated flank wear. The whole point is that the wedge has only 90 degrees to share among rake, clearance and wedge, so any gain in one direction is a loss elsewhere. This is why you do not dial clearance to taste on an indexable insert: the catalog's designation has already balanced it for the material and operation, and the operator's job is to understand why a too-large clearance chips the edge while a too-small one rubs. It also explains the recurring pattern in this module — positive rake, large nose radius, big lead angle, light hone — each reduces force and rubbing but weakens or vibrates the edge, and a strong interrupted cut wants the opposite family. Geometry is never "more is better"; it is choosing which trade the specific setup can afford.

Common Mistakes

  • Choosing the biggest nose radius for finish regardless of rigidity. It chatters on slender parts. Fix: size the nose radius to the setup, and use the smallest radius that meets the finish.
  • Using positive rake in a heavy interrupted cut. The weak edge chips. Fix: use negative rake / a stronger edge designation for roughing and interrupted cuts.
  • Thinking clearance is a free “more is better” angle. Excessive clearance weakens the wedge. Fix: use the catalog designation matched to the material and operation.
  • Reading geometry without the approach angle. Nose radius and lead angle together decide finish and radial force. Fix: read the full geometry designation, not one field.

Practice

Q1. What do α, β and γ sum to?

90 degrees.

Q2. A heavy, interrupted cut on hardened steel — positive or negative rake?

Negative rake: stronger edge that survives interruption and high feed.

Q3. f = 0.2 mm/rev, rε = 0.8 mm. Ideal Ra?

Ra = 0.04/(32×0.8) = 0.00156 mm = 1.56 µm (ideal only).

Q4. Why does a large nose radius risk chatter?

It increases the contact length and radial pushing force, which excites vibration on slender setups.
