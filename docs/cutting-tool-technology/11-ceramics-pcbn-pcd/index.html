Ceramics, PCBN & PCD

Beyond carbide lie the superhard materials: ceramic for high-speed steel and cast-iron machining, PCBN for hard turning, and PCD for non-ferrous and abrasive work. They cut fast and hold size, but they are brittle — and PCD must never touch steel.

Level: BPrerequisite: 10 Cemented CarbideScope: classroom overview
After this lesson, you can:
  • Name the main uses of ceramic, PCBN and PCD
  • State the brittleness and setup limits that apply to all three
  • Explain why PCD cannot machine steel

Concept

Ceramic inserts (alumina or silicon-nitride based) stay hard at very high temperature and run high speeds, but are brittle and dislike interrupted cuts and coolant shocks. PCBN (cubic boron nitride) is the second-hardest material after diamond, used to hard-turn steel above ~45 HRC and to machine hard cast irons. PCD (polycrystalline diamond) is the hardest tool material, used for aluminum, non-ferrous metals, composites and abrasive non-metals.

Why It Matters

These materials unlock work carbide cannot do: turning a hardened die to size without grinding, or finishing abrasive aluminum at high speed. But the same hardness means a light tap, a chatter, or a coolant blast can break the edge. The rule for superhard tools is: rigid setup, continuous cut, generous edge prep, and no surprise interruption.

How It Works

Superhard tool materials at a glance
MaterialBest atAvoid
Ceramichigh-speed turning/milling of steel and cast ironheavy interruption, thermal shock from coolant
PCBNhard turning >45 HRC, hard cast ironsoft steels (built-up edge), thin chatter-prone work
PCDhigh-Si aluminum, copper alloys, composites, plasticsany steel / ferrous (chemical diffusion)

PCD and iron are chemically incompatible at cutting temperature: carbon diffuses into iron, so a PCD tool on steel self-destructs. This is the single most important "never" in superhard tooling.

Worked Example

A hardened 55 HRC die insert needs a precise OD. Grinding is slow and rigid. Instead, hard-turn it with a PCBN insert on a rigid lathe, at high speed and light feed, in a continuous cut. The result replaces a grinding step. The same PCBN tool would be wrong on a soft 304 part (built-up edge), and a PCD tool chosen "because it is hardest" would destroy itself on the steel.

Classroom assumption: the 45 HRC threshold and hard-turning speed are general guidance; exact data comes from the PCBN/ceramic manufacturer for the grade and setup.

Matching the superhard tool to the material

These tools are chosen for one job and used nowhere else. PCBN turns hardened steel and cast iron at high speed where carbide would wear in minutes; it is brittle and demands a rigid machine, sharp edge preparation, and light interrupted loads. PCD machines non-ferrous, abrasive materials like aluminum and composites to high quality, but it is chemically attacked by iron at cutting temperature, so it is never used on steel. Ceramics run extremely fast on cast iron and heat-resistant alloys but are notch- and shock-sensitive. The selection rule is therefore material-first: hardened steel → PCBN; abrasive aluminum/composites → PCD; high-speed cast iron or superalloys → ceramic. Using the hardest tool on the wrong material wastes money and breaks fast.

Worked choice: three parts, three tools

Apply the material-first rule to three incoming jobs. Job one is a high-silicon aluminum housing with a good surface requirement — the abrasive silicon abrades plain carbide, and the material is non-ferrous, so PCD (or diamond-coated carbide for lower volume) is the answer. Job two is a 55 HRC hardened die that needs a precision OD after heat treat — that is the PCBN hard-turning case on a rigid lathe, replacing a grind. Job three is a large cast-iron brake disc run at very high speed in a continuous cut — ceramic, run dry or with air blast because flood coolant would thermal-shock the brittle edge. The wrong choices are instructive: PCD on the steel die would chemically dissolve, carbide on the silicon housing would wear in minutes, and coolant on the ceramic would crack it. Notice that "the hardest available" never appears in the reasoning — the material family, not the hardness rating, picks the tool.

Common Mistakes

  • Using PCD on steel. Chemical diffusion destroys the edge. Fix: PCD is non-ferrous only.
  • Treating superhard inserts like carbide. They chip under chatter and interruption. Fix: rigid setup, continuous cut, edge-checked before running.
  • Flooding a ceramic cut with coolant. Thermal shock cracks the edge. Fix: follow the maker's coolant guidance; dry or air-blast for ceramics.

Practice

Q1. Which material hard-turns 55 HRC steel?

PCBN (or ceramic for continuous high-speed work).

Q2. Why not PCD on steel?

Diamond carbon diffuses into iron at cutting temperature, wearing the edge away.

Q3. What setup condition do all superhard tools demand?

High rigidity and a continuous, non-chattering cut; they tolerate almost no interruption.
