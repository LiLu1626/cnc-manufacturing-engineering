Coating Processes & Families

A coating is a thin, hard layer bonded to the carbide edge: it blocks heat, resists wear and stops the workpiece welding to the rake face. CVD and PVD deposit it at very different temperatures, and TiN, TiCN and TiAlN each solve a different wear problem. Color tells you almost nothing — the chemistry does.

  • Compare CVD and PVD and when each is used

Concept

A coating is a very thin layer (typically a few micrometres) of hard, chemically stable material deposited onto a carbide (or HSS) substrate. It does three jobs: acts as a thermal barrier keeping heat away from the substrate; raises abrasive and crater wear resistance; and lowers friction / built-up edge by preventing the workpiece from welding to the edge. The two main deposition routes are CVD (chemical vapor deposition, hot, thick, good for roughing) and PVD (physical vapor deposition, cooler, thinner, keeps a sharp edge).

Why It Matters

The coating is often the difference between an insert that lasts 10 minutes and one that lasts an hour in the same cut — at little added cost. But it is paired with the substrate: a coating cannot rescue a brittle grade in an interrupted cut, and a thick CVD coating on a fine-finishing edge rounds it off. Picking the right coating family for the dominant wear mode (flank vs crater vs built-up edge) is what extends tool life, not just "put on a black coating."

Worked Example

Two milling jobs in steel. Job A roughs at high speed with heat and abrasive flank wear: a TiAlN PVD coating (hot-hard, sharp edge retained for interrupted milling) fits. Job B turns steel at moderate speed with long chip contact and crater wear: a CVD TiCN+Al2O3 layered insert (thick thermal/crater barrier) fits the continuous roughing cut. Picking the shiny black one because "it looks advanced" ignores which wear mode dominates.

Classroom assumption: thickness ranges and temperatures are typical; exact values vary by maker. The coating is matched to the substrate grade and operation, not chosen alone.

Matching coating to the job

A coating is a thin, hard, heat-resistant layer over the carbide substrate (page 10). CVD coatings are thicker, tougher and suit roughing and steel at high speed; PVD coatings are thinner, sharper and suit finishing, stainless and delicate edges. Common families — TiN (gold), TiCN, TiAlN — trade hardness, heat resistance and friction. The right coating lets you raise vc and extend life (page 29), but it is chosen by material group, not by color: a gold TiN that looks "premium" may be the wrong layer for a hard steel cut. Edge preparation (page 06) matters with coating too — an unhoned edge coated over can chip. Treat substrate, geometry and coating as one matched system selected together (page 13).

What a coating actually buys you

The payoff of the right coating is a step up in cutting speed for the same edge life, not just a longer-lasting insert. Take the locked baseline: uncoated carbide in the steel cut may be limited to a conservative vc because heat and crater wear attack the edge directly. A TiAlN or Al2O3-layered version of the same substrate acts as a thermal and chemical barrier, so you can raise vc — the speed that was cratering an uncoated edge now stays within the coating's window. The caution is that the coating is only as good as the system beneath it: if the substrate is too brittle for an interrupted cut, or the edge is hanging out with runout (page 22), the thin layer chips first and you are left blaming the coating. This is why page 13 treats substrate, geometry and coating as one matched choice rather than three independent levers. When comparing two catalog inserts, look at the material class and the recommended speed band for your workpiece — that band already encodes the coating benefit, and it is the number you plug into page 03 to get RPM.

Why the deposition temperature rounds a finishing edge

The CVD/PVD choice is not just about thickness. CVD runs at high temperature, and as the coating grows thicker (5–12 µm) it naturally rounds the micro-geometry of a sharp edge — a few micrometres of build-up blunts the theoretical corner. That is harmless for a robust roughing insert, where a slightly honed edge is actually desirable, but it is exactly what ruins a light finishing edge that needs to shear a clean wall. PVD deposits cooler and thinner (1–5 µm), so the edge comes out of the chamber close to the geometry it went in with, which is why finishing, milling and delicate cuts specify PVD even when the chemistry (TiAlN) could in principle be CVD. The generalisation is: thick, hot CVD is for strength, crater and heat in continuous roughing; thin, cool PVD is for keeping a sharp edge in interrupted or finishing work. It also interacts with edge prep (page 06) — a CVD edge effectively comes with an automatic micro-hone, so shops often pair CVD with a tougher substrate for roughing, and PVD with a light hone for finishing. Pick the process by whether your cut rewards edge sharpness or edge toughness, not just by coating chemistry.

Why the coating cannot rescue the substrate

It is tempting to treat the coating as the whole reason a modern insert survives, but the cross-section on this page shows the truth: the hard layer is only a few micrometres thick over a bulk of carbide that carries all the toughness. A coating wears through in a local spot — a chipped corner, a notch — and once it is breached, the bare substrate underneath takes the heat and abrasion directly, which is why coated and uncoated inserts often fail in the same morphology once the layer is gone. This is why a brittle substrate chosen for a hard, wear-resistant coating still chips in an interrupted cut: the thin skin adds wear resistance, not bulk impact strength. The selection logic is therefore layered: first choose a substrate with the right toughness for the interruption (page 10), then choose a coating that blocks the dominant wear mode on top of it, and never let a shiny coating justify a substrate too weak for the job. It also explains the diagnostic lesson of page 27 — if a coated edge is chipping rather than wearing, the fix is toughness and rigidity, not a different coating brand; the coating was never the weak link.

Why TiAlN survives high speed where plain TiN does not

The difference between TiN and TiAlN is not "TiAlN is just newer" — it is what happens to the coating when the edge runs hot. TiN is hard and low-friction at moderate temperature, but above a certain edge temperature it softens and begins to react with the steel chip, which caps how fast you can cut. TiAlN contains aluminium, and at cutting temperature it forms a thin, stable alumina-like film on the very surface of the coating: that film acts as a self-renewing thermal and chemical barrier, which is why TiAlN (and the higher-aluminium AlTiN) lets you run the high spindle speeds of modern dry milling. This is the mechanism behind the page-28 speed–life trade — the coating's hot hardness is exactly what permits a higher vc before cratering, and why high-speed steel milling and dry/hard machining lean on TiAlN rather than plain TiN. The practical limit is also honest: the self-protecting film only forms at temperature, so running TiAlN far too slowly wastes its advantage, and in gummy aluminium where that heat never develops, a simpler coating or even an uncoated sharp edge may be better. Match the coating's temperature window to the speed you actually run, rather than fitting the hottest coating to the slowest job.

Why aluminium and other non-ferrous materials want a different coating

The same thermal-barrier logic that makes TiAlN a high-speed winner on steel can work against it in aluminium. Aluminium is gummy and tends to weld to the edge (built-up edge, page 25), and it conducts heat well enough that the edge never reaches the temperature where TiAlN's protective alumina film helps. For high-silicon or abrasive aluminium, the dominant wear is abrasion by hard silicon particles, which is why PCD (page 11) — not another nitride — is the premium answer; for softer aluminium, an uncoated or a specially polished low-friction edge often cuts better than a hard steel-oriented coating, because the goal is to stop welding, not to block heat. This is why the coating choice is material-family-specific: the hot-hard TiAlN that lets you run steel at speed is neither necessary nor ideal on aluminium, and a shop that puts its favourite steel insert to work on an aluminium job may build up edge material and wonder why. Match the coating to the dominant wear of the actual material — heat and crater in steel, abrasion in high-silicon aluminium, built-up edge in gummy non-ferrous — rather than assuming the hottest coating is universally best.

Why a worn coating and a worn edge are diagnosed differently

Because the coating is only a few micrometres thick, its wear shows a characteristic sequence that helps diagnosis (page 27). A fresh coated insert has a smooth, coloured surface; as the layer wears through, the carbide colour begins to show through in a band on the flank — that is the normal wear land reaching the substrate, and it is the signal to index on schedule. If instead the coating flaks off in patches or the edge chips straight through the layer, that is not gradual wear: it points to an interrupted cut or a brittle setup that has fractured the thin skin, and the fix is edge toughness and rigidity, not a different coating. Reading the surface — uniform flank wear-through versus patchy spalling or edge chipping — tells you whether you are seeing normal end-of-life or a premature failure. This is why keeping the worn insert (page 27) matters even for coated tools: the pattern on the coating distinguishes "time to index" from "something is wrong," and it prevents a shop from buying a new coating brand when the real issue was runout or interruption.

Common Mistakes

  • Choosing by coating color. Shade is cosmetic. Fix: read the chemistry and the wear mode it addresses.
  • Expecting a coating to fix a bad substrate/setup. A coating on a brittle or overhanging tool still fails. Fix: coating is the final layer, not the cure-all.
  • Using a thick CVD edge for light finishing. The coating rounds the edge and hurts finish. Fix: use thin PVD for sharp finishing edges.

Practice

Q1. What three jobs does a cutting coating do?

Thermal barrier, wear resistance, and reduced friction/built-up edge.

Q2. Rough steel turning with crater wear — CVD or PVD?

CVD, often layered TiCN+Al2O3, for the thick thermal/crater barrier.

Q3. High-speed steel milling where edge sharpness matters — which process?

PVD, thinner and cooler, retaining a sharp edge.

Q4. Why not choose a gold "advanced" coating by eye?

Color varies by brand; the chemistry and wear-mode fit decide, not the look.
