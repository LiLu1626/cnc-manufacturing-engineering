Workpiece Materials & Machinability

Machinability is not a property stamped on a bar. It is the result of hardness, strength, toughness, work hardening, thermal conductivity and abrasiveness acting together — and it changes with heat-treat condition. This page turns those properties into the tool and speed choices they actually drive.

Level: KPrerequisite: 01 The Cutting Tool as a SystemScope: metric; classroom examples
After this lesson, you can:
  • Define machinability and the five properties that move it

  • Explain why hardness, work hardening and low thermal conductivity each change the tool recipe
  • Read a material data sheet and extract the numbers that matter to a tooling decision

Concept

Machinability is a relative measure of how easily a material can be machined to the required finish and tolerance at reasonable tool cost. There is no single unit. In industry it is usually expressed one of three ways: the cutting speed that gives a fixed tool life (say 15 minutes) under standardized conditions; the material removal rate achievable; or the resulting surface finish and chip behavior. All three are tied to a specific tool material — “machinable” with carbide may be “not machinable” with high-speed steel.

Five physical properties dominate:

  • Hardness — resistance to plastic deformation; directly raises cutting force and abrasive wear.
  • Toughness / strength — energy absorbed before fracture; strong, tough materials build up heat and resist shearing.
  • Work hardening — the material gets harder as it is deformed, common in austenitic stainless and aluminum alloys.
  • Thermal conductivity — whether heat leaves the chip zone through the part or stays at the edge.
  • Abrasiveness — hard particles in the microstructure (carbides, oxides, silica) that grind the edge.

Why It Matters

A tooling decision starts from the workpiece, not the tool. If you treat “steel” as one thing, you will choose a generic grade and lose tool life. Annealed low-carbon steel, quenched-and-tempered 4140 at 280 HB, and pre-hard H13 at 48 HRC are three different machining jobs even though a non-engineer calls all three “steel.” The heat-treat condition sets hardness, which sets whether you use a coated carbide grade, cermet, ceramic or PCBN — and each step down in speed window costs cycle time or tool cost.

The other reason is safety of assumptions. A bar labeled “aluminum” may be 6061-T6 (free cutting) or a high-silicon 390 aluminum (very abrasive to uncoated carbide). Recording the actual grade, condition and hardness — and its source — is what separates an engineering choice from a guess.

Reading the data sheet for the numbers that matter

A material cert or data sheet gives tensile strength, yield strength, elongation, hardness and heat-treat condition. For tooling, hardness and condition carry the most weight; tensile strength and elongation explain chip behavior; the grade tells you which wear family to expect. Record them, with their source, on the selection worksheet.

Worked Example

Two bars both say “steel.” Bar A is annealed 1018, ~120 HB. Bar B is quenched-and-tempered 4140, ~280 HB. Same nominal operation: face-turning a flange OD. If you choose the same carbide grade and the same speed, Bar B will wear the edge much faster because hardness has more than doubled and the chip carries more shear energy.

Classroom assumption: the 120 HB and 280 HB values are realistic typical values for those conditions, but the exact speed and wear numbers are invented for teaching. Real allowable speed must come from the insert catalog for the actual grade, coolant and setup.

The engineering response is not “run slower and hope.” It is: tougher substrate for the interrupted and higher-force cut, sharper controlled edge prep, possibly a CVD coating for crater resistance, and re-evaluating speed from the catalog — then confirming tool life with a short trial (page 28).

Why work-hardening materials punish light cuts

Austenitic stainless, many aluminum alloys and some superalloys work-harden: the very act of deforming the surface makes it harder. If the feed per tooth is too low, the edge does not shear a fresh layer but rubs over the already-work-hardened skin from the previous pass — and that skin is now harder than the bulk, so the tool grinds against a surface it created. This is the counter-intuitive trap on 304/316: a light, gentle “safe” finish pass actually wears the edge faster than a firmer one, because it never leaves the hardened zone. The response is to feed enough to cut beneath the work-hardened layer on each pass, keep the edge sharp (a worn edge rubs and work-hardens too), and avoid dwelling in the cut. This is why the property table on this page pairs “work hardening” with “higher feed, avoid rubbing, sharp edge” rather than with “reduce feed” — it is one of the clearest cases where the intuitive cautious move is exactly wrong, and it connects directly to the too-light-feed failure on page 04.

Why low thermal conductivity concentrates heat at the edge

Heat from the cut has two exits: out through the chip (which is good) and out through the workpiece or tool. In a good heat-conducting material like aluminum or mild steel, the part itself carries heat away, so the edge stays relatively cool even at high speed. In titanium, nickel superalloys and austenitic stainless, the material conducts poorly, so almost none of the shear heat leaves through the part — it stays right at the rake face where the edge is. That is why these families cannot run the high cutting speeds their hardness alone might suggest: it is not that they are too hard, it is that they are too insulating, and the edge temperature spikes until the coating softens and notch wear appears. The response is a heat-resistant coating (page 12), a positive geometry that shears rather than rubs, and generous coolant aimed at the edge (page 23), rather than simply pushing speed. This is also why the same coating on a titanium cut and on a mild-steel cut behaves differently — in titanium the coating is the only heat shield the edge has, so its hot hardness decides the whole allowable speed.

Why heat-treat condition matters more than the alloy name

Two of the same nominal alloy can machine like different families, because the heat-treatment condition sets the actual hardness. Annealed 4140 at ~200 HB turns at a normal coated-carbide speed; the same bar drawn and quenched-and-tempered to 300 HB, or pre-hard to 44 HRC, steps the job into a lower speed window with a tougher edge. This is why reading only "4140" on a drawing is not enough — the condition and hardness are what select the grade and speed, and they are why a tooling worksheet records "condition + hardness + source" rather than just the alloy number. It also explains why a part that machines beautifully in the soft state turns into a short-tool-life problem once it is heat-treated before finishing: the same operation, the same machine, a different material hardness. When a job comes back with "the tool suddenly wears fast," the first question is whether the incoming hardness or condition changed, not whether the grade deteriorated.

Why free-machining additives exist and what they cost

Some steels and brasses are deliberately alloyed with small additives — lead, sulfur in the form of manganese sulfide, or similar — that make chips break short and reduce built-up edge. These free-machining versions cut far more easily than their plain equivalents and are preferred for high-volume screw-machine work, which is why the family table lists free-machining low-carbon steel as medium-speed with good chip control. The trade-off is that free-machining alloys are slightly weaker and the hard sulfide inclusions can be abrasive to the edge in other ways, so they are not automatically "better" for every part. The practical reading is that the exact sub-alloy matters: a free-cutting 12L14 and a plain 1018 are both "low-carbon steel," but the first chips cleanly and tolerates a higher feed, while the second produces a stringy chip that needs a chipbreaker. Recording the actual grade on the worksheet is what tells you which of those two you are holding, and it prevents applying a free-machining recipe to a gummy plain bar.

Why cast iron cuts cool but leaves abrasive dust

Cast iron looks easy because it chips into small fragments and the graphite lets it flow with little built-up edge, so it tolerates high speeds and even uncoated carbide. But the fractured chip carries hard abrasive particles, and the machined skin abrades the flank, which is why cast-iron inserts wear by flank abrasion rather than by crater or built-up edge. The practical consequence is that the edge wants a wear-resistant rather than super-tough grade, and the fine, dusty chips need good extraction because they score surfaces. It is another case of reading the dominant wear from the material family — cast iron's problem is abrasion, not heat or welding — and matching the substrate and coolant strategy to that rather than to the alloy name alone.

Common Mistakes

  • Saying “machinability” without naming the tool. A material is only “easy” relative to a given tool material and condition. Fix: always state “machinable with carbide at this hardness.”
  • Ignoring work hardening. Taking a very light finish pass on 304 stainless that rubs rather than cuts, hardening the surface and accelerating flank wear. Fix: use a feed that actually shears, and keep the edge sharp.
  • Using free-cutting assumptions on abrasive alloys. Running uncoated carbide in high-silicon aluminum and expecting normal life. Fix: recognize abrasiveness and move to PCD or a wear-resistant grade.
  • Recording only the grade, not the condition. “4140” tells you nothing about hardness. Fix: record condition + hardness + source.

Worked machinability comparison. Take the same Ø10 end mill running the same body, but compare 6061-T6 aluminum against AISI 1045 steel. On aluminum the chip flows freely, forces are low, and vc can sit near 200 m/min; on the same geometry over 1045 the temperature and tool load are far higher, so a comparable edge lives at a much lower vc. Machinability is therefore not a single number — it is the combined effect of hardness, strength, thermal conductivity and work-hardening on the four things the tool feels: force, heat, chip shape and wear rate. When a job shifts between material groups (for example from P-steel to S-titanium), treat the whole cut as new: never carry over the previous vc, and re-confirm the chipbreaker feed window, because a chip that forms cleanly in steel can weld and pack in a gummy superalloy. Reading the material first, before touching a speed or feed, is what keeps the first cut safe.

Practice

Q1. List the five properties that drive machinability.

Hardness, toughness/strength, work hardening, thermal conductivity, abrasiveness.

Q2. Why does low thermal conductivity hurt the tool?

Heat cannot leave through the part, so it concentrates at the cutting edge, raising edge temperature and accelerating crater and notch wear.

Q3. Two bars are both labeled “aluminum.” What should you check before assuming the same speed?

The silicon content / alloy and condition. High-silicon (e.g. ~390) is far more abrasive and may need PCD rather than uncoated carbide.

Q4. What does “machinability” actually measure, and what is it always relative to?

A relative measure (speed for fixed tool life, MRR, or finish) — always relative to a specific tool material and cutting condition.
