Wear Mechanisms & Morphology

Tool wear is not one thing. Flank wear on the clearance face, a crater on the rake face, a built-up edge, a notch at the depth of cut, or plastic deformation — each has a different look and a different cause. Recognizing the morphology points to the fix.

Level: KPrerequisite: 04 Chip FormationScope: classroom overview
After this lesson, you can:
  • Identify the main wear types by where they appear
  • Explain the typical cause of each
  • Read a worn edge like a diagnosis

Concept

Flank wear is the gradual abrasion on the clearance (relief) face — the normal, expected wear that sets tool life. Crater wear is a hollow eroded into the rake face where the hot chip slides. Built-up edge (BUE) is workpiece material welded onto the edge. Notch wear is a groove at the depth-of-cut line. Plastic deformation is the edge squashing under heat and force.

Why It Matters

The wear pattern tells you what the cut is doing. Flank wear steadily degrades size — that's the normal end of life. A crater on the rake means the edge is too hot or the grade lacks crater resistance. BUE means the edge is rubbing and welding material, usually from too-low speed. Reading the edge before changing the tool points at the parameter or grade to fix, instead of just swapping inserts blindly.

Measuring the Wear Land

The flank wear land is measured on the relief face just below the cutting edge. Because the land often wears more at the depth-of-cut line (where notch wear forms), the average flank wear VB and the maximum localized wear VBmax are both reported. An insert may look acceptable on average while a notch at the cut line is already deep enough to ruin the surface. Inspect the edge under magnification or a tool microscope, and record the reading rather than judging by eye.

End-of-life is set by whichever limit is reached first: a defined VB (commonly around 0.3 mm for finishing, more for roughing), a surface-finish limit, a dimensional drift, or sudden chipping. The point is that the limit is defined before the run, so "dull" means the same thing every shift.

Worked Example

An insert in steel shows a smooth wear land on the clearance face — normal flank wear; you measure it and index at the limit. Another insert in a gummy stainless shows material welded to the nose (BUE) and a poor surface: speed is too low and the edge is rubbing. A third shows a deep crater on the rake: the grade needs more crater resistance or lower speed. Same tool, three diagnoses.

Classroom assumption: wear limits and appearances are general; exact end-of-life criteria come from the insert specification.

Reading the wear curve

The flank-wear curve has three stages, and reading them tells you whether the edge is being run correctly. In the first minutes there is a small break-in wear as the tiny asperities of the new edge flatten; then wear settles into a long, gentle steady-state slope where most of the parts should be produced; finally wear accelerates sharply as the edge work-hardens, heats up and goes blunt. The practical rule is to index during the steady-state region — before the final spike. If a brand-new edge reaches the wear limit after only a few parts, the break-in stage never settled: speed, feed or engagement is too aggressive for the grade. If wear is perfectly linear and slow, the edge is running in its window. If the surface suddenly deteriorates part-way through a supposedly normal life, suspect a spike from BUE or chipping rather than smooth flank wear. Measuring VB at intervals on the trial log turns the curve from a picture into the scheduled-change point on page 30.

Why notch wear hides in the VBmax number

Average flank wear VB is measured across the middle of the land, but the deepest wear often concentrates right at the depth-of-cut line — the notch — where the exiting chip and any surface scale or work-hardened layer abrade the edge. That notch can be far deeper than the average land while the middle still looks acceptable, which is why inspection reports both VB and VBmax. It matters because the notch sits exactly where the fresh surface is being cut: a deep notch leaves a groove mark on the part, and the rough edge at the notch is the usual origin of a later chip or fracture. The cause is typically the work-hardened skin left by the previous pass or a workpiece scale, so the corrective direction is not "lower speed everywhere" but reducing the boundary engagement, changing entry/exit, or choosing a notch-resistant geometry — the same mapping used on page 27. This is also why judging wear by the average land alone can let a notch ruin finish long before the measured VB reaches its limit.

Why BUE can look protective but actually ruins the edge

Built-up edge is a paradox worth understanding, because it tricks new operators. At low cutting speed in a gummy material, the workpiece does not flow cleanly along the rake face; instead a layer of it welds onto the edge and becomes a hard, sintered cap that temporarily takes the cutting on its behalf. From a distance this can look like the edge is still sharp, and in very early stages the BUE cap may even reduce direct flank contact. But the cap is not stable: it periodically breaks off, and when it does it rips a fragment of the real carbide coating or substrate away with it — that is how a BUE-ridden edge actually wears, and why the finished surface comes out torn and spotty. The welded cap also pushes the effective cutting geometry outward, so the part cuts oversize and the finish degrades. This is why BUE is treated as a low-speed problem to be removed (raise vc within grade limits, improve lubricity to the rake face, use a sharper or smoother geometry), never as a protective layer to be left alone. It also explains the connection to page 04: the same low cutting temperature that allows welding is the root cause, and raising speed moves the edge into the regime where the chip flows instead of welding.

Why the crater is a temperature map of the edge

The crater does not erode at random: it digs into the rake face exactly where the hot chip slides and reaches its peak temperature, a little way back from the edge rather than right at it. That location is diagnostic in itself. If you see a pronounced hollow on the rake, the edge is running hot enough that the chip–tool contact zone is softening or chemically attacking the grade — the corrective direction is thermal, which is why the table pairs crater wear with "too hot / low crater resistance" and not with feed or rigidity. Two levers respond: lower vc (which drops the temperature roughly with speed) or choose a grade with better crater resistance — typically a harder, more heat-resistant substrate and a CVD coating built for high-temperature steel cutting (page 12). This is the mirror image of BUE: BUE says the edge is too cold and welding, a crater says it is too hot and eroding, and a quick look at the nose tells you which side of the temperature window you are on. Reading the morphology this way — flank wear is normal, BUE is too slow, crater is too hot, notch is at the work-hardened boundary — turns a worn insert into a one-page diagnosis, which is the entire point of pages 25–27.

Why you define the wear limit before the run

The reason the page asks you to index at a defined VB rather than when the edge "looks dull" is that without a written limit, "dull" means a different thing to every operator and every shift. One person changes at the first sign of a rough surface, another runs the insert until it screams, and the resulting parts-per-edge jumps around so much that page 29's cost math has no dependable input. A defined limit — say VB = 0.3 mm, or a finish threshold, or a dimensional drift — is agreed before the trial, then measured at intervals on the log (page 28) and the edge is changed the moment whichever limit is hit first. This also makes the steady-state region the planned production zone: you deliberately replace before the accelerating tail, because running into the spike both shortens the usable edge and risks a sudden chip that ruins the part. The morphology diagnosis on this page only feeds that decision — once you know it is normal flank wear rather than BUE or a crater, the fix is scheduling, not parameter changes; and the measured VB is the number that feeds the cost-per-part calculation later.

Why plastic deformation means the grade is overmatched

Plastic deformation is a wear mode that looks less like wear and more like the edge being physically squashed: the tip bulges or rounds under heat and force, and the edge ceases to hold its geometry. It happens when the grade's hot hardness is simply below what the cut demands — the carbide softens at temperature and yields under the cutting pressure, rather than abrading away. This is why it is the signature of an overmatched substrate: running a medium steel grade on hardened steel, or pushing vc so high that even a coated grade reaches its softening point. The corrective direction is therefore not a tweak — it is a fundamentally more heat-resistant grade (page 11, ceramics/PCBN for hard turning), a lower speed, and often a more robust edge prep. Distinguish it from flank wear because the action differs: a worn land means "index on schedule," while a deformed nose means "this grade cannot survive this cut at all." Seeing the edge flatten or the corner roll instead of wear steadily is the trigger to step up the substrate class, not to run the same tool a little longer.

Why a fresh edge needs a break-in run

The wear curve's first stage — a short, slightly elevated wear as the brand-new edge settles — is why operators sometimes ease into a new edge rather than slamming straight into full parameters. A perfectly honed new insert has tiny, sharp micro-asperties that flatten in the first few minutes; if you start at maximum speed and feed on a hard interrupted cut, those fragile asperties chip before they bed in, and the edge's whole life is shortened. This is the logic behind a gentle break-in: a short period at slightly reduced speed and feed lets the edge polish itself into its steady-state wear pattern, after which it runs to the planned VB. It also explains why a new edge that fails almost immediately may have been abused at break-in rather than being a bad insert. In production the break-in is short and built into the first parts, but it matters most for expensive, fragile PVD or superhard edges — and it is one more reason to measure VB at intervals, so the break-in region is recognised rather than mistaken for a defective grade.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating all wear as "the tool is dull." Fix: identify the wear type to target the cause.
  • Ignoring BUE as normal. It ruins finish. Fix: raise speed / change geometry.
  • Waiting for total failure. Fix: index at a defined flank-wear limit.

The skill this page builds is separating wear from overload: a gradual, uniform flank land with normal edge shape is predictable wear you can plan a life around, while a sudden brittle chip or a heat-cracked rake points at a condition that will recur until the setup or speed changes. Always name the morphology before naming the cause.

Practice

Q1. Where does flank wear appear?

On the clearance (relief) face; it is the normal wear land.

Q2. Material welded to the nose — what and why?

Built-up edge, usually from too-low speed in a gummy material.

Q3. A crater on the rake face suggests?

High heat / low crater resistance; adjust speed or grade.
