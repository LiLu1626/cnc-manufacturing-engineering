Wear Mechanisms & Morphology

Tool wear is not one thing. Flank wear on the clearance face, a crater on the rake face, a built-up edge, a notch at the depth of cut, or plastic deformation — each has a different look and a different cause. Recognizing the morphology points to the fix.

Level: KPrerequisite: 04 Chip FormationScope: classroom overview
After this lesson, you can:
  • Identify the main wear types by where they appear
  • Explain the typical cause of each
  • Read a worn edge like a diagnosis

Concept

Flank wear is the gradual abrasion on the clearance (relief) face — the normal, expected wear that sets tool life. Crater wear is a hollow eroded into the rake face where the hot chip slides. Built-up edge (BUE) is workpiece material welded onto the edge. Notch wear is a groove at the depth-of-cut line. Plastic deformation is the edge squashing under heat and force.

Why It Matters

The wear pattern tells you what the cut is doing. Flank wear steadily degrades size — that's the normal end of life. A crater on the rake means the edge is too hot or the grade lacks crater resistance. BUE means the edge is rubbing and welding material, usually from too-low speed. Reading the edge before changing the tool points at the parameter or grade to fix, instead of just swapping inserts blindly.

Measuring the Wear Land

The flank wear land is measured on the relief face just below the cutting edge. Because the land often wears more at the depth-of-cut line (where notch wear forms), the average flank wear VB and the maximum localized wear VBmax are both reported. An insert may look acceptable on average while a notch at the cut line is already deep enough to ruin the surface. Inspect the edge under magnification or a tool microscope, and record the reading rather than judging by eye.

End-of-life is set by whichever limit is reached first: a defined VB (commonly around 0.3 mm for finishing, more for roughing), a surface-finish limit, a dimensional drift, or sudden chipping. The point is that the limit is defined before the run, so "dull" means the same thing every shift.

Worked Example

An insert in steel shows a smooth wear land on the clearance face — normal flank wear; you measure it and index at the limit. Another insert in a gummy stainless shows material welded to the nose (BUE) and a poor surface: speed is too low and the edge is rubbing. A third shows a deep crater on the rake: the grade needs more crater resistance or lower speed. Same tool, three diagnoses.

Classroom assumption: wear limits and appearances are general; exact end-of-life criteria come from the insert specification.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating all wear as "the tool is dull." Fix: identify the wear type to target the cause.
  • Ignoring BUE as normal. It ruins finish. Fix: raise speed / change geometry.
  • Waiting for total failure. Fix: index at a defined flank-wear limit.

Practice

Q1. Where does flank wear appear?

On the clearance (relief) face; it is the normal wear land.

Q2. Material welded to the nose — what and why?

Built-up edge, usually from too-low speed in a gummy material.

Q3. A crater on the rake face suggests?

High heat / low crater resistance; adjust speed or grade.
