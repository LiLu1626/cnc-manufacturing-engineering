Reaming, Boring & Hole Quality

A drilled hole is near-net, not accurate. Reaming finishes and sizes a hole to a fixed diameter; boring corrects position and roundness. Choosing between them — and leaving the right stock — decides whether the hole meets its tolerance and finish.

Level: KPrerequisite: 17 DrillsScope: metric; classroom examples
After this lesson, you can:
  • Distinguish reaming and boring and when each is used
  • Set a reasonable reaming stock allowance
  • Explain why boring follows drill position errors
  • Name the limits of each process

Concept

Reaming passes a multi-tooth reamer through a drilled hole to size it to a fixed diameter and improve finish. It follows the existing hole's alignment — it cannot move the hole's center. Boring uses a single-point boring bar on a lathe or machining center to cut the hole to a chosen diameter, correcting position and roundness from the previous operation. Drilling → boring → (reaming) is the usual hole-making ladder.

Why It Matters

The two processes solve different problems. If a drilled hole is roughly right in position but needs a closer diameter and better finish, ream it. If the hole wandered off center or is not round, boring is the only way to correct it — reaming would just polish the mislocated hole. Leaving too much stock for reaming loads the reamer and makes it wander; leaving too little leaves drill marks. The allowance is the whole trick.

Stock allowance

Reaming removes only a small amount — typical radial stock ~0.1–0.3 mm for a medium drill size. More than that overloads the reamer; less leaves drill marks. Boring removes a deliberate rough then finish amount to control size.

Worked Example

A drawing calls for a Ø10 H7 hole with a good finish. Sequence: drill Ø9.7 mm, then ream to Ø10. Radial stock left for reaming is (10−9.7)/2 = 0.15 mm — a healthy small allowance. If the hole must also be precisely located relative to a datum, bore it after drilling instead, because reaming cannot move the center. Boring depth is limited by the bar overhang logic from page 16.

Classroom assumption: the 9.7/10 mm sequence and 0.15 mm allowance are typical; exact values depend on material, hole size and reamer manufacturer.

Common Mistakes

  • Reaming a mislocated hole to fix position. Reaming follows the hole. Fix: bore first to correct position.
  • Leaving too much stock for reaming. The reamer overloads and wanders. Fix: keep the small allowance.
  • Expecting reaming on a deep slender bore. Bar rigidity limits it. Fix: control overhang and rigidity first.

Practice

Q1. Hole wandered off center — ream or bore?

Bore, to correct position; reaming cannot move the center.

Q2. Drill Ø14, ream Ø14.5. Radial stock?

(14.5−14)/2 = 0.25 mm radial.

Q3. Why leave only a thin layer for reaming?

A reamer is a sizing/finishing tool; heavy stock overloads it and breaks accuracy.
