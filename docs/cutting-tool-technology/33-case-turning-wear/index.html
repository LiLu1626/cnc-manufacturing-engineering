Case: Turning Wear Diagnosis

A lathe insert fails prematurely. Following the method from page 27, we read the edge, name the wear type, isolate the cause, change one variable, and verify — instead of just swapping inserts.

Level: KPrerequisite: pages 25–28Scope: worked case
After this lesson, you can:
  • Apply the diagnosis method to a real turning failure
  • Distinguish wear, BUE and chipping in the evidence
  • Record the corrective action and verification

Concept

This case walks the page-27 loop: observe the worn insert, separate gradual wear from premature failure, match morphology to cause, change one variable, verify. The lathe operation is OD turning of a stainless bar.

Why It Matters

Premature insert failure in production costs money per stop. A structured diagnosis turns a recurring nuisance into a fixed parameter or grade, and the record feeds tool management (page 30).

How It Works

Observe: the insert shows built-up edge on the nose and a poor finish, not a smooth wear land. Separate: premature — the edge is rubbing and welding material. Hypothesize: speed too low for the work-hardening stainless. Change one thing: raise cutting speed within grade limits (and confirm chipformer). Verify: BUE reduces and finish improves; tool life logged on the trial sheet.

Worked Example

The same method would branch differently by evidence: a crater on the rake points to hot/crater wear (adjust grade or speed), chipping points to shock (tougher grade/rigidity). Here the welded nose says low speed, not overload. Each branch changes a different lever.

Classroom assumption: the stainless and BUE scenario is illustrative; confirm the speed adjustment within the insert catalog's range for the grade.

Reading the record

The worn insert from the stainless bar shows a built-up nose and a torn-looking finish, while the flank land itself is modest — so this is not an insert that wore out from long service. The diagnosis (page 27) points to low speed in a work-hardening material, not to a weak grade. The single change is raising vc within the catalog's stainless range and confirming the chipformer is suited to gummy flow. On the follow-up run, BUE should shrink and the finish improve; if instead crater or flank wear accelerates, the speed was pushed too far and the grade should step up. The result goes on the wear-trial log so the next stainless job starts at the corrected speed, not at the old guess.

What the follow-up tells you

The verify step is what turns a guess into knowledge, and it has three possible outcomes. If BUE drops and the finish cleans up, the hypothesis was right and the corrected speed becomes the new starting point for this material. If raising speed makes BUE better but crater wear or a red-hot chip appears immediately, the speed was pushed past what the current coating survives — the next change is a more heat-resistant grade, not more speed. If nothing changes at all, the welded nose was not really BUE: look again at the edge, and suspect a chip not breaking and rewelding, or a feed too low for the chipformer window — the next lever is feed and chipbreaker, not speed. Writing down which outcome occurred is the point of the trial log: the next person does not have to re-derive it.

Putting numbers on the speed change

To make the "raise speed" lever concrete, translate it to RPM rather than leaving it vague. Suppose the stainless part is turned on an Ø60 bar and the old, BUE-producing speed was a cautious 80 m/min. From n = 1000·vc /(π·D) that is 1000×80 /(π×60) = 424 rpm. The catalog's recommended band for a PVD-coated stainless grade might be 120–160 m/min; moving to 140 m/min gives 1000×140 /(π×60) = 743 rpm — a 75% step up, but still inside the catalog window. This is why the case says "raise speed within grade limits" rather than "make it faster": the number is anchored to the catalog band and the diameter, not chosen by feel. Notice also that raising vc on a fixed diameter raises RPM in exact proportion, so the spindle speed command and the chip evacuation both change at once — another reason to keep feed and depth fixed during the trial, so the only thing that changed is the temperature at the edge where BUE forms. If 743 rpm already throws heat or crater wear, the grade step-up (page 12) is the next move, not backing off speed.

Why you rule out setup before changing the grade

The reason the diagnosis loop has a “rule out” step is that many premature-failure complaints are not about the edge at all. A loosely seated insert, a built-up chip in the pocket, a wrong tool offset, or a holder that is not drawing the insert fully home all look from the outside like a tool that does not last: the finish goes off, the size wanders, the edge seems to wear fast. If you skip the setup check and jump straight to ordering a more expensive grade, you have spent money on a hypothesis that was never tested — and the new insert fails the same way. So the first action on a recurring failure is the cheapest one: confirm the insert seats cleanly, the pocket is free of swarf, the offset and nose-radius compensation are correct, and the bar is rigid. Only after those are known-good do you read the wear morphology and change a process variable. This is the same zero-cost-first principle as page 27: every setup check costs minutes, every new grade and every trial costs money and stops the machine. Diagnose the cheap causes first, then spend.

Why the corrected speed is recorded, not just remembered

The point of the wear-trial log is that a solved problem this week should not reappear as a mystery next month. When the corrected speed (the 743 rpm from 80 m/min on the Ø60 bar) is written against the grade, insert geometry and material, the next person loading that stainless bar starts at the right speed and never experiences the welded-nose failure that cost the first batch. Without the record, knowledge lives in one operator's head and evaporates when the shift changes; with it, the shop accumulates a small, true database of "what actually worked here" instead of re-deriving every setting from the catalog each time. This is exactly the link to tool management on page 30: individual diagnoses become shop-wide starting points. A rule of thumb worth keeping — if you had to run a trial to find it, write it down, because the reason you had to trial it is that the catalog band alone was not specific enough for your machine, material and coolant. The log is what turns one lucky fix into a repeatable process.

What each failure mode would have looked like instead

To make the diagnosis sharper, it helps to hold the alternatives side by side. If the edge instead showed a smooth, even flank land that grew steadily until finish failed, that would be normal wear at too-high speed — the fix is to lower vc or take a more wear-resistant grade, the opposite lever to the welded-nose case. If it showed a small crescent crater on the rake face at high temperature, that is crater wear from the long steel chip sliding at speed, again pointing at speed and grade rather than setup. If it showed a chipped corner with a rough, interrupted or scale-covered entry, that is mechanical shock and wants a tougher substrate and a more rigid setup, not a speed change. Only the shiny, welded nose with a torn finish says "speed too low in a work-hardening material." Practising this matching — holding the morphology in your hand and asking which of these four stories it tells before reaching for a tool — is what turns page 27's loop from a procedure into a reflex, and it is why the worn insert is photographed or kept rather than thrown away: it is the evidence that decides which lever to turn.

Why changing only one variable keeps the diagnosis honest

The loop says "change vc only, keep feed and depth fixed" because a trial that moves speed and feed together cannot tell you which lever helped — and worse, it can move two levers that pull in opposite directions, so the result looks unchanged and you abandon the right fix. Raising speed reduces BUE but tends to heat the edge, while raising feed thins the chip and changes the chipbreaker window; if you do both at once and the finish improves a little, you never learn which one mattered for next time, and if it gets worse you do not know which to back off. The one-variable rule is what makes the verify step meaningful: the before-and-after difference is attributable to the single thing you changed. This is also why the corrected speed on the log is trustworthy — it was isolated, not bundled. On the shop floor this sometimes feels slow, but it is the difference between a diagnosis that reproduces and a set of parameters that happened to work once and cannot be reasoned about next time. Keep the diameter, feed and depth constant during the trial, change the one lever the morphology points to, and let the measured VB and finish decide the next move.

Why stainless rewards a sharper, freer edge at the corrected speed

Once the speed is raised out of the BUE regime, the geometry choice quietly matters too. Work-hardening stainless does not shear like plain steel: a negative-rake, blunt edge pushes and rubs, inviting the very welded layer the diagnosis just removed, even at a correct speed. This is why the case also says "confirm the chipformer" — the corrected speed is paired with a geometry that shears rather than smears, usually a sharper positive-rake edge with a chipbreaker tuned to gummy flow and a PVD coating that stays smooth. Raising speed alone helps, but pairing it with the freer edge is what makes the finish clean rather than merely less welded. This is the same system view as page 12: substrate, coating and geometry are chosen together, and a speed correction is rarely the whole fix. If after raising speed the BUE is reduced but the edge still drags, the next lever is geometry and chipbreaker window, not a further speed increase.

Why this case is deliberately not about a broken tool

The scenario — a welded nose, a torn finish, but an intact insert — is chosen on purpose, because the most expensive recurring failures on a lathe are quiet rather than dramatic. A snapped insert stops the machine immediately and gets attention; a slowly welding edge that produces torn parts and short, unpredictable life instead drifts along, and the cost shows up in rejected pieces and edge changes nobody planned. The structured loop exists to catch exactly this quiet case before it becomes a scrap issue. It also illustrates the diagnostic habit the course is building: reach for the morphology first, not for the alarm. A shiny nose and a bad finish are not a crisis, they are a readable sign pointing at one lever, and treating them as information rather than annoyance is what lets you correct the speed once instead of replacing edges all week. The dramatic fractures are covered on page 26; this case is the everyday, low-noise problem most shops actually live with.

Common Mistakes

  • Reaching for a tougher grade when BUE is the issue. BUE is speed-related. Fix: read the morphology first.
  • Changing speed and feed at once. Fix: one variable, then verify.

Practice

Q1. Welded material on the nose suggests what?

Built-up edge, usually too-low speed in a gummy/work-hardening material.

Q2. What is the single first change?

Raise speed within grade limits, then re-observe BUE and finish.
