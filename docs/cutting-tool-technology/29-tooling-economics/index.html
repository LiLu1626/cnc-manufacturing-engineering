Tooling Economics

The cheapest insert is often the most expensive choice. Tooling cost should be compared per finished component, balancing insert cost, machine hour rate and tool life. A pricier grade that lasts much longer often wins — this page does the math.

Level: KPrerequisite: 28 Tool Life TestingScope: classroom example
After this lesson, you can:
  • Estimate tooling cost per component
  • Compare two tool options on cost per part
  • Explain why insert price alone is the wrong comparison

Concept

The real comparison is cost per good component, not cost per insert. Two terms matter: the tool cost spread over parts, and the machine time the tool consumes (machine hour rate × cycle). A tool that costs more but lasts twice as long, or cuts faster and shortens cycle, can lower cost per part — even at a higher sticker price.

Why It Matters

Buying the cheapest insert saves cents on the tool but loses dollars on machine time and scrapped parts. Conversely, a premium grade paid for only if it measurably extends life or raises material removal. The locked case below shows why the cheaper-looking option can lose.

How It Works

Approximate tooling cost per part = tool cost spread over good parts, plus the downtime of indexing over those parts. The locked CTT baseline uses two competing options, A and B, at 0.64 vs 0.525 cost units per part — the seemingly pricier tool B wins because its cost spread over more good edges and less frequent indexing, not because the inputs prove it cuts faster.

Worked Example

Two turning options on the same part, with the same assumed machine-hour rate. Option A: 120 CU insert, 4 usable edges, 50 good parts per edge. Option B: 160 CU insert, 4 usable edges, 80 good parts per edge. A: 120 / 4 / 50 = 0.60 CU per part, plus allocated indexing downtime of 0.04 CU, giving 0.64 CU per part. B: 160 / 4 / 80 = 0.50 CU per part, plus indexing downtime of 0.025 CU, giving 0.525 CU per part. The difference is 0.115 CU per part. These inputs do not establish a shorter cutting cycle for B; any difference in cutting-cycle cost or scrap cost must be measured and added separately.

Classroom assumption: the 0.64 / 0.525 figures are the locked CTT baseline for teaching. Substitute your own insert price, machine hour rate, tool life and cycle time for a real decision.

Cost per part, not price per edge

The cheapest insert is often the false economy. What matters is the cost per finished part: insert price divided by parts per edge, plus the machine time and any scrapped-part risk. The locked example shows it: option A (cheap edge, short life) works out to 0.64 cost units per part, option B (pricier edge, longer life) to 0.525 — B wins despite the higher sticker price. Speed enters this too: running faster shortens cycle time but also shortens tool life faster (page 28), so there is an optimum rather than a "fastest is best." A valid comparison holds machine rate, insert cost and tool-life data on the same page, which is exactly what the cost-per-part calculation forces you to do.

How the comparison is built up

The cost-per-part number comes from two streams added together. First, the tool stream: the cost of one edge divided by the number of good parts that edge produces, which spreads a fixed insert price over its life. Second, the indexing stream: the machine downtime of changing an edge, spread over the good parts that edge produces. A cheap insert with short life spreads its small price over few parts, and its edge-change downtime lands on few parts too — that is how option A lands at 0.64. A pricier edge that makes more good parts per edge spreads its larger price over more parts, and its less frequent indexing lowers the downtime share — landing at 0.525. Notice what is not concluded here: these inputs do not say B has a shorter cutting cycle. Run that comparison separately with your real cycle time, machine rate and scrap data, and remember that a tool that occasionally scrapes a part adds rework and downtime that no insert quote includes.

Why the machine rate often dominates

In most CNC shops the machine hour rate — depreciation, labor, overhead — dwarfs the insert price, which changes how the cost-per-part math should be read. Suppose an edge costs a few cost-units and produces hundreds of parts: its tool stream per part is fractions of a unit, while a cycle that runs a minute longer adds a full minute of the machine rate on every single part. That is why a tool that lets you raise vc and shorten cycle time can win even if it wears faster and costs more per edge: you are buying back machine time, which is the expensive commodity. It also explains the opposite trap on a lightly-loaded, low-rate machine — a cheap, long-life insert may win because the machine time saved by a faster premium tool is worth little there. The comparison is therefore always local to your shop: plug your real machine hour rate and measured parts-per-edge into the two streams, and do not assume the premium tool wins universally. When the rate is high and the machine is the bottleneck, cycle-time reduction rules; when the rate is low and the machine has spare capacity, edge life and price dominate.

Why scrap and unplanned downtime are hidden tool costs

The two streams — tool price spread over parts, and machine time per cycle — capture the steady case, but the cost-per-part comparison is quietly biased unless you also price the failures. A cheaper edge that occasionally builds up edge material and leaves a torn finish may pass the average-cost math yet quietly trigger rework, inspection and a scrapped part every few hundred pieces; a broken edge in an interrupted cut stops the machine until someone finds and changes it. Those unplanned stops are not in the insert quote, and because the machine rate is the expensive term (as the previous section explains), even a rare downtime event outweighs the few cents saved on the edge. This is why the "marginal tool that scrapes parts" warning appears in the mistakes: reliability of the edge — predictable wear, no surprise chipping, no walk-off — is itself an economic variable, not just a quality nicety. When you compare options in real data, include the cost of rework, the downtime from unexpected edge failure, and the inspection burden a borderline finish creates; otherwise the cheap tool looks cheaper than it really is, and the first scrapped batch erases every saving.

Why there is an optimum speed, not a fastest speed

Raising vc pulls the two cost streams in opposite directions. Faster cutting shortens the machine-time stream directly, because each part cycles sooner — good when the machine rate dominates. But by the Taylor relation (page 28), higher speed collapses tool life, so the tool stream — insert price spread over fewer parts — rises. At very low speed the edge lasts forever but the machine crawls, bleeding rate per part; at very high speed the cycle is short but edges vanish and downtime from changes dominates. The lowest cost per part sits somewhere in between, and that is the speed you should run, not the fastest the spindle allows. This is why a blanket "always cut faster" is wrong, and why the locked 0.64 vs 0.525 case is about a balanced option rather than the absolute fastest one. Finding that optimum is what a small speed trial (page 28) actually delivers: a couple of speed points, the parts-per-edge at each, and the cycle times — plotted onto the two streams, the minimum cost point becomes visible instead of guessed.

Why you need real parts-per-edge data, not catalog figures

The tool stream in this page divides edge price by the number of good parts one edge actually makes, and that divisor is the number most often guessed wrong. The catalog's stated tool life is an ideal value under ideal, rigid conditions; on your machine, with your fixture, overhang and coolant, the real parts-per-edge can be well below it — or, if your setup is unusually stiff, above it. Using the catalog number in the cost-per-part math therefore imports an assumption you have not tested, and the whole comparison is only as good as that divisor. This is why page 28 exists before this one: a short life trial on the actual job gives you the real parts-per-edge for options A and B, and only then does the 0.64 vs 0.525 comparison mean anything. Running the cost math on catalog life while the shop quietly experiences shorter life is how a "cheaper" option wins on paper and loses on the floor. Measure the divisor, keep it honest, and the cost-per-part result becomes a decision rather than a forecast.

Why edge changes themselves cost money

The cost-per-part math counts insert price spread over parts and machine time per cycle, but a third, easy-to-forget stream is the edge-change event itself. Every time an edge reaches its wear limit, the machine stops: someone walks over, loosens the clamp, indexes or replaces the insert, re-sets the seat, and the spindle sits idle for those minutes. On a high-volume part, that downtime happens on a schedule — if an edge lasts half an hour, that is two stoppages an hour — and because the machine rate is the expensive term, those stoppages can outweigh even the insert price. This is another reason a longer-life grade can win beyond the sticker price: it changes edges less often, which buys back not just wear but the mechanical work of changing over. It also explains why a very cheap grade that requires changing every few minutes is quietly costly on a bottleneck machine, even at zero insert cost. When you build the comparison, count not just parts per edge but also the minutes of spindle downtime each edge change consumes, and the longer-life option usually looks even better.

Why tooling decisions are revisited as volume changes

The winning option is not fixed for a part's whole life — it shifts with production volume and machine loading. In a small prototype or one-off batch, the expensive premium grade is hard to justify: you will never make enough parts to recover its price, and a cheap insert that lasts the handful of cuts is the rational choice. Once the same part becomes a high-volume runner on a bottleneck machine, the machine-time stream dominates and the longer-life, faster premium tool pays back quickly. This is why a tooling decision made at the quote stage should not be frozen forever: as volume rises, re-run the cost-per-part comparison, because the divisor (parts per edge) and the relative weight of the two streams change. It is also why stocking two grades for the same feature is reasonable rather than indecisive — the cheap one for low-volume trials, the productive one for the long run. Treat the 0.64 vs 0.525 result as valid for a given volume and rate, and re-test it when either changes substantially rather than carrying the old winner forward.

Why the locked numbers are illustrative, not a universal answer

The 0.64 versus 0.525 result is a teaching case, not a rule that the premium grade always wins. In your own shop the winner depends entirely on the two real inputs: the machine hour rate and the measured parts-per-edge. On a high-rate bottleneck machine, cycle-time reduction tilts the balance toward the faster, pricier tool; on a low-rate machine with spare spindle capacity, the cheap long-life insert may win outright. The point of the locked numbers is to show the method — build the two streams, divide edge cost by real parts, add machine time — and then substitute your own figures. Treat B winning here as an example of how the arithmetic works, not as a reason to buy the expensive grade on every job. When the numbers are rerun with real shop data, a different option may legitimately win, and that is the expected outcome rather than a contradiction.

Common Mistakes

  • Comparing insert prices, not cost per part. Fix: include life and machine time.
  • Ignoring scrap and downtime. A marginal tool that scrapes parts costs far more. Fix: price in quality cost.
  • Buying the fastest tool regardless of price. Fix: only the cost-per-part math justifies it.

Practice
