Cemented Carbide Substrates

Cemented carbide is tungsten-carbide particles held together by cobalt. Grain size and cobalt content are the two dials: fine grain and low cobalt resist wear but are brittle; coarse grain and higher cobalt absorb shock but crater sooner. This page reads those dials and matches a grade family to a job.

Concept

Cemented carbide is a cermet-like composite: hard tungsten carbide (WC) grains are sintered with a cobalt (Co) metal binder. The WC grains provide hardness and wear resistance; the cobalt binder provides toughness by holding the grains together. Manufacturers vary two main levers — grain size (fine to coarse) and cobalt content — and add titanium/tantalum carbides for steel-machining grades.

Why It Matters

Not all carbide is the same. A “wear” grade for finishing aluminum and cast iron is brittle and will chip on a steel interruption; a “tough” grade for roughing steel will crater too fast in a long finishing pass. The grade designation in the catalog is the manufacturer's position on these two dials. Reading that position tells you whether the insert can survive your interruption and hold up to your speed.

How It Works

Cobalt content

More cobalt = tougher and shock-absorbing, but softer at high temperature and more prone to crater/wear. Less cobalt = harder and more wear-resistant at temperature, but more brittle. Steel grades also add cubic carbides (TiC/TaC/NbC) to resist cratering from the long chip contact in steel turning.

The two broad grade families

Industry groups carbide into P/M/K-style application classes (ISO): P = steel, M = stainless/alloy, K = cast iron/non-ferrous. Within each, the catalog pushes you toward wear-resistant (speed, finish) or shock-resistant (roughing, interruption) depending on the operation.

Worked Example

A shop runs two operations on the same part. (1) Finish boring a steel bore at high speed to a tight tolerance — continuous cut, needs edge retention: a fine-grain, lower-cobalt, steel grade with coating resists crater wear. (2) Rough facing a forging with heavy scale and interrupted entry — shocks the edge: a tougher, higher-cobalt grade survives the impacts even though it wears a little faster. Choosing one grade for both either chips the finishing edge or wears out the roughing edge.

Classroom assumption: grain-size cutoffs and cobalt ranges are general; exact grades are proprietary. Match the operation to the catalog's wear-vs-tough recommendation.

What grain size buys you

Cemented carbide is hard tungsten-carbide particles held in a cobalt binder. Fine-grain grades wear slower and hold a sharper edge, but are more brittle; coarse-grain grades are tougher for interrupted cuts and roughing. More cobalt binder adds toughness but reduces hardness and hot strength. The substrate is the "body" onto which a coating is applied (page 12), so choosing it is a toughness-vs-wear trade: roughing and interrupted cuts → tougher, coarser; finishing hard material → finer, harder. Most shop-floor carbide is graded by application (steel / stainless / cast iron) precisely so you do not have to derive the grain-binder balance yourself — the catalog already encodes it.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating all carbide as the same. A finishing grade chips on roughing. Fix: pick wear vs toughness by operation, not just material.
  • Running a tough roughing grade at high finishing speed. It craters. Fix: use a finer, more wear-resistant grade for continuous finishing.
  • Ignoring the ISO application class (P/M/K). A K-grade for cast iron is not ideal for steel. Fix: match the grade class to the workpiece family.

Practice

Q1. What holds the WC grains together?

Cobalt metal binder, sintered into the structure.

Q2. Fine grain trades what for what?

More wear resistance and sharpness, against lower toughness.

Q3. Why does roughing with scale want higher cobalt / coarser grain?

It absorbs shock and thermal cycling better; the edge survives interruption even if it wears faster.

Q4. What do steel grades add to resist cratering?

Cubic carbides such as TiC/TaC/NbC in the substrate.
