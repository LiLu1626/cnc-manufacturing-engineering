Tool Material Families

The substrate is what survives at the edge: HSS, carbide, cermet, ceramic, PCBN, PCD. They trade hardness for toughness along one spectrum — harder lasts longer at speed but breaks easily, tougher forgives abuse but cannot run fast. This page places each family and tells you when you reach for it.

Level: KPrerequisite: 05 Rake & ClearanceScope: classroom overview
After this lesson, you can:
  • Place HSS, carbide, cermet, ceramic, PCBN and PCD on the hardness/toughness spectrum
  • State the main advantage and limitation of each family
  • Match a family to a material and operation at a high level

Concept

Tool materials are ordered by their ability to stay hard at the temperature of the cut (hot hardness) and their toughness (resistance to fracture). The central trade-off: the harder the material, the faster it can cut, but the more brittle it is.

  • HSS (high-speed steel) — tough, cheap, resharpened; low speed.
  • Cemented carbide — the workhorse; balanced hardness/toughness, used coated or uncoated.
  • Cermet — harder, sharper, for high-speed finishing of steel.
  • Ceramic — very hot-hard, for high-speed and hardened steel, brittle.
  • PCBN (cubic boron nitride) — hardest after diamond, for hard turning >45 HRC.
  • PCD (polycrystalline diamond) — hardest, for non-ferrous/abrasive non-metals; never on steel.

Why It Matters

Picking a substrate is the first materials decision after you know the workpiece. Running carbide at HSS speeds wastes productivity; running ceramic in an interrupted cut snaps it. PCD on steel chemically diffuses and destroys the edge — an expensive mistake. Knowing which family fits the material and hardness window prevents both scrap and broken tooling.

Worked Example

Three jobs. (1) Finish-turning 55 HRC hardened steel: carbide will wear fast, but PCBN or ceramic can hard-turn it. (2) Milling abrasive high-silicon aluminum: uncoated carbide abrades quickly, PCD lasts far longer. (3) A form tool on an old manual lathe: HSS is cheap and resharpened, and the low speed does not demand hot hardness. The substrate choice follows the material and hardness, not the other way around.

Choosing a tool-material family

The broad order of hardness and heat resistance, from toughest-but-softer to hardest-but-brittle, is: high-speed steel → cemented carbide (the workhorse) → ceramics → PCBN/PCD. Roughing and interrupted cuts need toughness, so carbide with a tough grade wins; high-speed finishing of hardened or abrasive materials pushes you toward PCBN or ceramic. Coating (page 12) on a carbide substrate is the everyday upgrade that lets you run faster and last longer without buying a superhard tool. Most CNC machining uses coated carbide because it balances speed, wear resistance and toughness across steel, cast iron and stainless. Reach for the exotic families only when the material or operation specifically demands them — as page 11 spells out — because they are brittle, expensive and unforgiving.

Why coated carbide dominates the shop

If carbide is the workhorse, coated carbide is why it stays that way. A thin CVD or PVD film (page 12) on a carbide substrate raises hot hardness and lowers friction, so the same edge runs faster and resists crater and flank wear without losing the substrate's toughness. That is the everyday upgrade: instead of jumping from HSS to a brittle ceramic, most CNC operations step from uncoated to coated carbide, gaining productivity while keeping interruption tolerance. The decision sequence on the shop floor is therefore almost always: start with a coated-carbide grade matched to the material group, and only move to cermet/ceramic/PCBN when the material hardness, speed requirement or abrasiveness specifically exceeds what coated carbide can give — which is exactly the hard-turned 55 HRC and abrasive-aluminum cases on this page. Buying PCD or PCBN "just in case" for ordinary steel roughing is the mirror mistake: an expensive brittle tool in a job coated carbide does better.

Why PCD on steel is a chemistry problem, not a hardness problem

It is tempting to think "diamond is the hardest, so it should cut anything" — but PCD on steel fails fast for a chemical reason, not a mechanical one. At cutting temperature, carbon (the diamond edge) diffuses into iron, which is a strong carbon solvent; the edge steadily dissolves into the chip rather than staying sharp. This is why the superhard, longest-lasting tool for aluminum and composites is essentially useless on steel, and why the rule "PCD never on ferrous metal" is absolute rather than a tuning preference. The matching superhard material for hardened steel is PCBN, which is not carbon and does not suffer that iron reaction. The lesson generalizes: tool-material selection is not ordered by hardness alone but by chemical compatibility with the workpiece at cutting temperature. Aluminum loves PCD because it does not react and because PCD resists the built-up edge that gums carbide; steel needs carbide, cermet, ceramic or PCBN, none of which diffuses into it. When a PCD edge mysteriously self-destructs on a ferrous cut, this is the mechanism.

Why hot hardness, not room hardness, decides speed

The reason a ceramic or PCBN edge can run so much faster than HSS is not how hard it sits on a bench at room temperature — it is how hard it remains at the temperature of the cutting edge, which easily reaches several hundred to over a thousand degrees Celsius in a high-speed cut. HSS is hard enough at room temperature to machine steel, but it softens sharply once the edge gets hot, which is exactly why it is limited to low cutting speeds: push vc up and the edge reaches its tempering temperature and collapses. Carbide keeps its hardness to a much higher temperature, and ceramic and PCBN higher still, so they can run the high surface speeds that productivity demands. This reframes the whole spectrum on this page: you are not ranking materials by static hardness but by how much of their hardness they retain while red-hot under load. It also explains why "the edge got hot and deformed" is a hot-hardness failure (page 25, plastic deformation), and why raising speed without a material that survives the heat does not buy productivity — it just softens the tool faster.

Common Mistakes

  • Using PCD on steel. Diamond diffuses with iron at temperature and the edge fails. Fix: PCD is for non-ferrous only.
  • Expecting HSS to run at carbide speeds. It softens and dulls instantly. Fix: reserve HSS for low-speed/form work.
  • Using brittle ceramic/PCBN in interrupted cuts. They chip. Fix: use tough carbide for interruption; save superhard grades for continuous cuts.

Practice

Q1. Which family is the general CNC workhorse?

Cemented carbide (coated, for most steel/iron work).

Q2. Hard turning 55 HRC — which family?

PCBN or ceramic, not plain carbide.

Q3. Why not PCD on steel?

Diamond reacts/diffuses with iron at cutting temperature and the edge degrades rapidly.

Q4. What happens as hardness rises along the spectrum?

Hot hardness and allowable speed rise, but toughness/resistance to fracture falls.
