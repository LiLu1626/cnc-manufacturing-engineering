Final Assessment

The course ends with a practical assessment: knowledge questions, record reviews, and two real selection/diagnosis projects. It is designed like the rest of the course — applied, not rote. Aim to justify every answer, not just recall it.

Level: KCovers: modules M1–M7Scope: self-assessment

Concept

The assessment has three parts, scored out of 100: basic questions (40) testing the core concepts, record reviews (20) checking you can read a worn tool / selection record, and two projects (40) requiring a written tool selection and a wear diagnosis. Pass when you reach the threshold and every key technical point is correct.

Why It Matters

Recalling that "G00 is rapid" proves little. Applying the locked numbers, choosing a grade for a material, and diagnosing a worn edge prove you can do the job. The projects mirror the way tooling decisions actually happen on the shop floor.

Worked Example (sample questions)

  1. Locked baseline: Ø10 tool, vc = 120 m/min. Compute spindle RPM. (Answer: n = 1000·vc /(π·D) = 3819.72 RPM.)
  2. n = 3800, z = 4, fz = 0.04 — feed rate? (608 mm/min.)
  3. Material welded to the insert nose — name it and give the first corrective direction. (Built-up edge; raise speed within grade limits.)
  4. Ø10 drill at 118° through a plate — point height to add? (3.004 mm.)

Record reviews and the two projects use the printable worksheets: fill a selection card and a wear-trial log, then justify each choice.

How the locked numbers are derived

The basic-question arithmetic is the same on every locked example; learn it once and it repeats. Spindle speed from cutting speed: n = 1000·vc / (π·D). With vc = 120 m/min and D = 10 mm that is 1000·120 / (3.1416·10) = 3819.72 RPM — you would run it down to about 3800 RPM on the machine. Feed rate then is vf = n·zc·fz = 3800·4·0.04 = 608 mm/min. Surface finish from feed f and nose radius rε: Ra ≈ f²/(32·rε) = 0.10²/(32·0.4) = 0.781 µm. And the drill point for a 118° drill: h = (D/2)·tan((90−118/2)°) = 3.004 mm of extra depth beyond the measured hole. If you can reproduce these four by hand, the numeric basic questions are secure.

Worked scoring example

How the threshold actually decides. Suppose a candidate scores 34/40 on the basic questions, 16/20 on the record reviews, and 32/40 on the two projects — a total of 82. The raw score is above 80, so it would pass on points alone. But the pass rule has a second condition: every key technical point must be correct. If that same candidate computed the locked RPM as 380 (a factor-of-ten unit slip) or called built-up edge a flank-wear problem, the key-point condition fails despite 82 points. Conversely, a candidate on 84 points who reproduces all four locked numbers and names the wear modes correctly passes. The two conditions are both required: the points confirm breadth, the key points confirm that the essentials are not guessed. This is why memorizing a list is not enough — the arithmetic and the cause-mapping have to be right.

What the two projects actually ask for

The 40 project points are not essays — they are structured deliverables on the worksheets. The selection project gives you a short brief (a material, a feature, a tolerance, a quantity) and asks you to fill the selection card: read the drawing and material, choose operation and geometry, choose substrate and coating, set starting vc/fz from the catalog, and check holder/overhang/coolant — each field justified, exactly the page-13 workflow. The diagnosis project gives you a description of a failing edge (a welded nose, a checked rake, a chipped corner) and asks you to name the wear mode, separate wear from overload, state the single first change, and describe how you would verify it on the wear-trial log — exactly the page-27 loop. Grading is against the same rubric used through the course: a correct tool choice with no justification earns partial credit, and a correct number with the wrong unit earns none. Treat the projects as mini versions of cases 32 and 33, not as new material.

Why a right number with the wrong unit still fails

The rule "a correct number with the wrong unit earns no points" is not pedantic — on the shop floor the unit error is the accident. Compute the locked feed as 608 and enter 608 inches per minute instead of mm/min, and the tool moves about 25 times too far and fast, crashing the cut. Drop the ×1000 in the RPM formula and get 3.8 instead of 3800, which looks like a decimal slip but is a thousand-fold speed error. These are exactly the failures the course drills against (page 03): the arithmetic is the easy part; the unit bookkeeping is where a real program goes wrong. That is why the assessment weights units and asks you to reproduce the locked conversions by hand, and why the practice questions deliberately mix mm/min, RPM and µm. A candidate who can derive 3819.72 RPM but cannot say which diameter or speed produced it has not actually learned the system — they have memorised an answer. The key-point condition exists to catch that gap, not to be difficult.

How to use a failed assessment as a study map

The point of the two-condition pass rule is not to gate-keep; it is to tell you exactly what to re-learn. If you score 82 but fail a key point, look at which one: a slip on the RPM formula means revisit page 03 and re-derive n, vf and Ra by hand until it is automatic; naming built-up edge as flank wear means re-read pages 25–27 until the morphology-to-cause mapping is instant; a selection project that picked a brittle grade for an interrupted cut means revisit pages 09–13 and re-run case 32. Each failed key point points at one chapter, not at "the whole course." This is why the assessment is best treated as a diagnostic you can take more than once: the first attempt exposes the weak link, you study that chapter specifically, and a second attempt confirms it closed. A candidate who skips the practical half because recall feels easy, or who memorises the four locked numbers without understanding their units, learns the wrong lesson — the assessment rewards the same applied judgement the shop requires, and retaking it after targeted review is how the module actually turns into skill.

Common Mistakes

  • Answering numbers without units. Fix: always state mm/min, RPM, µm.
  • Describing a tool choice without justification. Fix: link it to material and operation.
  • Skipping the record review. Fix: the projects and record reviews carry 60 of 100 points — a perfect recall score still fails the practical half.

How to prepare

Work through the locked examples by hand before the assessment: compute 3819.72 RPM from vc and diameter, compute 608 mm/min feed, convert f and nose radius to 0.781 µm, and recall the 3.004 mm drill point. Be ready to explain why a higher-price insert can win on cost per part (page 29), and to name the wear mode from a description (page 25/27). The two projects use the printable worksheets — fill them as if for a real job, justifying each field rather than guessing.
