Case: Milling Tool Selection

Putting it together: select a milling tool for a real pocket-and-profile job using the workflow from page 13, the geometry rules from M2, the substrate/coating logic from M3, and the setup checks from M4.

Level: KPrerequisite: pages 13–24Scope: worked case
After this lesson, you can:
  • Walk the full selection workflow on one job
  • Justify geometry, grade, coating and setup choices
  • Record the decision on the selection worksheet

Concept

This case applies the whole module to one component: a steel rectangular part with an open pocket and a finished side profile, moderate quantity. We follow drawing → material → operation → geometry → substrate/coating → parameters → setup.

Why It Matters

Pieces of knowledge are only useful when combined. A single selection that justifies each choice — rather than guessing at the shelf — shows the method works end to end and gives a repeatable record.

How It Works

  1. Drawing: open pocket, Ra 1.6 finish wall, square corners, moderate volume.
  2. Material: plain carbon steel, continuous cut, chip-forming.
  3. Operation: rough pocket then finish wall and floor.
  4. Geometry: corner-radius end mill for the finished wall; a roughing mill for stock.
  5. Substrate/coating: steel-grade carbide, PVD TiAlN for the interrupted milling heat.
  6. Parameters: start from catalog; the locked Ø10, n=3800, fz=0.04 → vf=608 baseline sets the scale.
  7. Setup: shortest holder, low runout, through/flood coolant.

Worked Example

Following the steps: rough with a chip-splitting roughing end mill, then finish with a corner-radius mill at the locked baseline feed. The selection worksheet records why: corner radius for chip-resistant corners, TiAlN for heat in steel milling, short holder for rigidity. That record is the deliverable.

Classroom assumption: the part and numbers are illustrative; confirm exact tools and speeds from the catalog for the actual steel grade.

Decision walk-through

Start from the drawing: a mild-steel fixture plate needs face milling, light profiling and several drilled holes. Walking page 13: material is free-cutting steel → coated carbide is appropriate, no need for superhard. Roughing needs a robust, multi-flute indexable face mill with a moderate lead angle; the light profiling uses a solid carbide end mill with a few flutes for chip clearance. Because the machine is a standard VMC, keep overhang short and rely on a rigid ER holder. The first-pass vc and fz come from the catalog for P-steel; the run then confirms them. The point of the case is not the exact tool number — it is that every choice traces back to the drawing, material and setup rather than to what happens to be in the drawer.

Verifying the selection on the first parts

A written choice is only a hypothesis until the first parts prove it. Run a short trial at the catalog starting values — the locked baseline vf = 608 mm/min sets the scale — then check three things: the finished wall reaches Ra 1.6, the edge survives a reasonable number of pockets without chipping, and the tool does not chatter at the chosen overhang. If the wall marks, suspect the finish tool or chip load first (page 04), not the coating. If the corner chips in the pocket corners, the interrupted entry needs a tougher edge prep or a slightly lighter engagement. If the machine chatters, rigidity (page 21) — shorten overhang or reduce ae — before changing the grade. Record the outcome on the selection worksheet so the next fixture plate starts from proven values rather than a fresh guess.

Why two tools, not one

A natural shortcut on this job is to rough and finish with the same corner-radius mill. It looks like it saves a tool change, but it forces a compromise: the same edge must either take heavy stock at a feed that risks chipping the finished wall, or finish gently after leaving too much stock for the roughing pass. Splitting the operation lets each tool do what it is good at. The roughing mill removes volume fast — high ae/ap, chip-splitting flutes, edge toughness over finish — and the corner-radius finisher then takes a light, uniform wall stock at the locked vf = 608 mm/min to reach Ra 1.6. The productivity math follows from MRR (page 36): roughing spends its time at high volume per pass, finishing spends its time at low volume but high surface quality, and trying to serve both with one tool pushes the whole cycle toward the slower, more fragile of the two regimes. The tool change between them costs seconds; a re-worked wall costs the whole part.

Why the pocket corner sets the tool diameter upper limit

A square (or small-radius) pocket corner imposes a hard mechanical limit that beginners miss: the end mill's own center cannot get closer to the corner than its radius, so the smallest inside corner you can leave equals the tool radius. If the drawing calls for a tight pocket corner and you chose the largest-diameter mill for stiffness, you literally cannot reach the corner — you leave a radius larger than specified, and the part is wrong even though every cut was clean. This is why the corner radius on the drawing is read before you fix the tool diameter, and why a finisher is often a smaller corner-radius mill than the roughing cutter: the rough tool can be large for volume, but the finisher must be small enough to follow the wall and reach the inside corner, within the rigidity limits of a smaller diameter. It is also why a true sharp internal corner on a milled pocket usually needs an EDM or a small corner-finishing tool rather than a bigger end mill. In this case the Ra 1.6 wall and the pocket corner radius therefore set the finisher size jointly, not the machine's biggest available holder.

Why the worksheet decision is a starting point, not a finished number

The selection you write down is the best hypothesis from the catalog, not a proven production setting. Catalog speed and feed are recommendations for ideal, rigid conditions on a well-maintained machine; your actual spindle power, fixture rigidity, overhang and coolant are never exactly those conditions. So the first pocket on this fixture plate should start at the catalog values — the locked vf = 608 mm/min baseline is exactly that kind of starting point — and then be adjusted by what the first few parts show: a clean wall and a tool that holds life means the values are usable; chatter means rigidity (page 21), not a softer grade; a marked wall means chip load or edge prep (pages 04–06), not the coating. Writing the decision down matters because it gives you something to compare the trial against; running the trial matters because it converts the recommendation into a number tuned to your machine. The worksheet therefore has two columns for a reason — recommended and actual — and the gap between them is the real engineering learning. Do not expect the catalog to be right on the first pocket, and do not discard the recommendation on the first failed one either; measure, change one thing, and record both.

Why you check clamping and power before the first cut

The tool selection on this page is only half the setup; the other half is whether the machine and fixture can deliver the cut you chose. Before running the first pocket, confirm two things that no tool choice can compensate for. First, the fixture holds the plate against both the cutting force and the upward tendency of climb milling — a fixture that lifts under load ruins both the wall and the cutter, and no grade fixes it. Second, the spindle has enough power for the chosen MRR: if the machine lugs down when the roughing mill enters, you cannot keep the locked vf and the catalog speed regardless of how good the tool is, and the realistic response is to step ap or ae down rather than push the spindle. These checks sit at the start of the worksheet because they are cheap (a look at the clamp, a quick power estimate from the force×speed logic on page 04) and they decide whether the whole tool recommendation is even runnable on this machine. A selection that is perfect on paper but needs 15 kW on a 5 kW VMC is not a usable recommendation; record the power and clamp assumptions the same way you record the tool, and the next setup starts with those constraints already visible.

Common Mistakes

  • Skipping justification. Fix: fill every step on the worksheet.
  • Finishing with the roughing tool. Marks the wall. Fix: separate rough and finish tools.

Practice

Q1. Why a corner-radius end mill for the finished wall?

It resists corner chipping and gives a controlled radius.

Q2. Locked baseline feed rate?

vf = n·zc·fz = 3800·4·0.04 = 608 mm/min.
