Case: Milling Tool Selection

Putting it together: select a milling tool for a real pocket-and-profile job using the workflow from page 13, the geometry rules from M2, the substrate/coating logic from M3, and the setup checks from M4.

Level: KPrerequisite: pages 13–24Scope: worked case
After this lesson, you can:
  • Walk the full selection workflow on one job
  • Justify geometry, grade, coating and setup choices
  • Record the decision on the selection worksheet

Concept

This case applies the whole module to the frozen CTT-M1 part: a 6061-T6 aluminum plate 80×60×15 mm with an open pocket 40×30×8 mm, inside corners R5, finished wall and floor, moderate quantity. We follow drawing → material → operation → geometry → substrate/coating → parameters → setup.

Why It Matters

Pieces of knowledge are only useful when combined. A single selection that justifies each choice — rather than guessing at the shelf — shows the method works end to end and gives a repeatable record.

How It Works

  1. Drawing: open pocket 40×30×8, inside corners R5, finished wall and floor.
  2. Material: 6061-T6 aluminum, gummy but low cutting forces, built-up-edge risk.
  3. Operation: rough the pocket volume, then finish wall and floor.
  4. Geometry: Ø8 mm, 3-flute solid carbide end mill; a 3-flute mill clears chips better than many flutes in aluminum, and Ø8 reaches the R5 corner (tool radius 4 < 5).
  5. Substrate/coating: submicron carbide, polished/bright PVD edge — aluminum is machined at high speed, and a smooth edge resists built-up edge rather than relying on heat-resistant coating.
  6. Parameters: vc = 200 m/min, fz = 0.04 mm/tooth. n = 1000×200/(π×8) = 7958 rpm; vf = n·zc·fz = 7958×3×0.04 ≈ 955 mm/min. At ap = 2, ae = 1.6: Q = ap·ae·vf/1000 = 2×1.6×955/1000 ≈ 3.06 cm³/min.
  7. Setup: shortest holder, low runout, flood or through-coolant to flush long aluminum chips.

Worked Example

Following the steps: rough the pocket at ap = 2, ae = 1.6 on the Ø8 3-flute mill, then a light wall and floor pass to finish. The selection worksheet records why: 3 flutes for chip clearance in aluminum, Ø8 because its 4 mm radius still reaches the R5 corner, polished edge to resist built-up edge, short holder for rigidity. Pocket volume after the four R5 corners is about 9.43 cm³ — at ~3.06 cm³/min the roughing time is the order of three minutes plus air moves. That record is the deliverable.

Classroom assumption: 6061-T6, vc = 200, fz = 0.04 and the pocket dimensions are locked CTT-M1 teaching values; confirm exact tools and speeds from the catalog for the actual machine.

Decision walk-through

Start from the drawing: the 6061-T6 plate needs the 40×30 pocket roughed and its wall/floor finished. Walking page 13: aluminum is soft and gummy, so a polished-edge solid carbide end mill at high speed is appropriate — no need for a heavy coated steel grade or superhard. The pocket volume is removed with a Ø8 3-flute mill at ap = 2, ae = 1.6; the 3-flute count balances chip clearance against feed at this small diameter. Because the machine is a standard VMC, keep overhang short and rely on a rigid ER holder. The vc = 200 and fz = 0.04 are catalog starting values for aluminum; the run then confirms them. The point of the case is not the exact tool number — it is that every choice traces back to the drawing, material and setup rather than to what happens to be in the drawer.

Verifying the selection on the first parts

A written choice is only a hypothesis until the first parts prove it. Run a short trial at the catalog starting values — the CTT-M1 start vf ≈ 955 mm/min sets the scale — then check three things: the finished wall reaches Ra 1.6, the edge survives a reasonable number of pockets without chipping, and the tool does not chatter at the chosen overhang. If the wall marks, suspect the finish tool or chip load first (page 04), not the coating. If the corner chips in the pocket corners, the interrupted entry needs a tougher edge prep or a slightly lighter engagement. If the machine chatters, rigidity (page 21) — shorten overhang or reduce ae — before changing the grade. Record the outcome on the selection worksheet so the next aluminum pocket starts from proven values rather than a fresh guess.

Why two tools, not one

A natural shortcut on this job is to rough and finish with the same corner-radius mill. It looks like it saves a tool change, but it forces a compromise: the same edge must either take heavy stock at a feed that risks chipping the finished wall, or finish gently after leaving too much stock for the roughing pass. Splitting the operation lets each tool do what it is good at. The roughing mill removes volume fast — high ae/ap, chip-splitting flutes, edge toughness over finish — and the corner-radius finisher then takes a light, uniform wall stock at a light wall pass near the CTT-M1 vf ≈ 955 mm/min to reach Ra 1.6. The productivity math follows from MRR (page 36): roughing spends its time at high volume per pass, finishing spends its time at low volume but high surface quality, and trying to serve both with one tool pushes the whole cycle toward the slower, more fragile of the two regimes. The tool change between them costs seconds; a re-worked wall costs the whole part.

Why the pocket corner sets the tool diameter upper limit

A square (or small-radius) pocket corner imposes a hard mechanical limit that beginners miss: the end mill's own center cannot get closer to the corner than its radius, so the smallest inside corner you can leave equals the tool radius. If the drawing calls for a tight pocket corner and you chose the largest-diameter mill for stiffness, you literally cannot reach the corner — you leave a radius larger than specified, and the part is wrong even though every cut was clean. This is why the corner radius on the drawing is read before you fix the tool diameter, and why a finisher is often a smaller corner-radius mill than the roughing cutter: the rough tool can be large for volume, but the finisher must be small enough to follow the wall and reach the inside corner, within the rigidity limits of a smaller diameter. It is also why a true sharp internal corner on a milled pocket usually needs an EDM or a small corner-finishing tool rather than a bigger end mill. In this case the Ra 1.6 wall and the pocket corner radius therefore set the finisher size jointly, not the machine's biggest available holder.

Why the worksheet decision is a starting point, not a finished number

The selection you write down is the best hypothesis from the catalog, not a proven production setting. Catalog speed and feed are recommendations for ideal, rigid conditions on a well-maintained machine; your actual spindle power, fixture rigidity, overhang and coolant are never exactly those conditions. So the first pocket on this plate should start at the catalog values — the CTT-M1 vf ≈ 955 mm/min start is exactly that kind of starting point — and then be adjusted by what the first few parts show: a clean wall and a tool that holds life means the values are usable; chatter means rigidity (page 21), not a softer grade; a marked wall means chip load or edge prep (pages 04–06), not the coating. Writing the decision down matters because it gives you something to compare the trial against; running the trial matters because it converts the recommendation into a number tuned to your machine. The worksheet therefore has two columns for a reason — recommended and actual — and the gap between them is the real engineering learning. Do not expect the catalog to be right on the first pocket, and do not discard the recommendation on the first failed one either; measure, change one thing, and record both.

Why you check clamping and power before the first cut

The tool selection on this page is only half the setup; the other half is whether the machine and fixture can deliver the cut you chose. Before running the first pocket, confirm two things that no tool choice can compensate for. First, the fixture holds the plate against both the cutting force and the upward tendency of climb milling — a fixture that lifts under load ruins both the wall and the cutter, and no grade fixes it. Second, the spindle has enough power for the chosen MRR: if the machine lugs down when the roughing mill enters, you cannot keep the locked vf and the catalog speed regardless of how good the tool is, and the realistic response is to step ap or ae down rather than push the spindle. These checks sit at the start of the worksheet because they are cheap (a look at the clamp, a quick power estimate from the force×speed logic on page 04) and they decide whether the whole tool recommendation is even runnable on this machine. A selection that is perfect on paper but needs 15 kW on a 5 kW VMC is not a usable recommendation; record the power and clamp assumptions the same way you record the tool, and the next setup starts with those constraints already visible.

Why climb milling changes the setup requirement

When the roughing mill enters the wall, the direction of cut — climb (up) versus conventional (down) — is not a style choice and it interacts directly with the rigidity checks on this page. In climb milling the chip starts thick and thins to zero, which saves edge wear and gives a cleaner wall, but the cutter wants to pull itself into the work and pushes the fixture and spindle against any backlash in the feed screws. That is why climb milling demands a machine and fixture stiff enough to resist that pull — which is exactly why the case checks clamping before the first cut. On an older machine with backlash, conventional milling spreads the load differently but rubs more at the start of each tooth and wears the edge faster. The practical consequence for the selection is that the modern, preferred climb strategy assumes the rigid setup this page has already verified; if the machine has noticeable backlash or the fixture lifts, the cheaper answer is not a stronger insert but to fall back on conventional milling or tighten the setup. This is one more reason the tool choice cannot be separated from the machine it runs on — the same geometry, run climb on a rigid VMC and conventional on a weak knee mill, gives different edge life regardless of the grade.

Why the floor and the wall are finished differently

The pocket has two finished surfaces — the vertical wall and the horizontal floor — and they do not share one cutter motion. The wall is finished by the tool's side teeth taking a light, uniform radial step, which is why the corner-radius end mill and a controlled ae matter. The floor is finished by the tool's bottom corner, and a mill that reaches the wall cannot also scrape the floor flat if its bottom geometry or its overhang deflects. This is why the case treats them as two finish cuts rather than one: the wall pass controls the side finish and the corner radius, and a separate floor pass (or the same tool at a different engagement) takes the last light depth on the pocket bottom. If you try to reach both with one side-and-bottom motion at the locked vf, the wall tends to mark and the floor to dish, because the tool is deflecting radially under the same pass that is trying to cut axially. The drawing calls out Ra on both, so the worksheet records a wall finish and a floor finish as separate deliverables, each with its own light stock and check — the same step-localised logic as the hole-quality case on page 34.

Why record the rejected alternative, not just the winner

A strong selection worksheet does not only list the tool you chose; it also notes the plausible tool you turned down and why. In this case the obvious alternative is a single large end mill doing both rough and finish (rejected, because it compromises both), and a high-flute-count mill for faster feed (rejected here, because too many flutes pack aluminum chips in the narrow pocket rather than clearing them). Writing those down matters for two reasons. First, it keeps the next person from re-proposing them as if they were new — the "more flutes = faster" suggestion, in particular, will recur. Second, it makes the reasoning inspectable: when the job later moves to a less gummy material or a flood coolant that clears chips, the high-flute alternative suddenly becomes reasonable, and the record shows exactly what changed (chip evacuation), rather than treating the new choice as a fresh guess. A selection that justifies only its winner is a recipe; one that records the rejected options is a decision history that adapts when the part changes.

Common Mistakes

  • Skipping justification. Fix: fill every step on the worksheet.
  • Finishing with the roughing tool. Marks the wall. Fix: separate rough and finish tools.

Practice

Q1. Why can an Ø8 mill reach the R5 inside corner?

The tool centre stays one radius (4 mm) from the wall; 4 < 5, so it can follow the R5 corner. A tool larger than R10 (radius >5) could not.

Q2. CTT-M1 feed rate?

n = 1000×200/(π×8) ≈ 7958 rpm; vf = n·zc·fz = 7958×3×0.04 ≈ 955 mm/min.
