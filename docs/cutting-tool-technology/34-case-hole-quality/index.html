Case: Hole Quality

A tight-tolerance hole needs a sequence, not a drill. This case plans drill → (bore or ream) with point depth, stock allowance and concentricity handled correctly.

Level: KPrerequisite: pages 17–18Scope: worked case
After this lesson, you can:
  • Plan a hole-making sequence to tolerance
  • Account for drill point depth and reaming stock
  • Choose bore vs ream by position and tolerance needs

Concept

This case chains drill (page 17) with reaming/boring (page 18): size a near-net drilled hole to a tolerance, while planning the conical point and the small reaming stock. Concentricity and position decide whether boring is required.

Why It Matters

A hole that drills to size but wanders, or that ignores the 118° point, either fails inspection or leaves a conical bottom. Planning the sequence up front avoids both.

How It Works

Required hole: Ø10 H7, good finish, located to a datum. Step 1: drill Ø9.7 (leaving ~0.15 mm radial stock, page 18). Step 2: if position must be corrected, bore; if the drilled hole is aligned and only needs sizing, ream. Add the 118° point height for through holes (page 17: h = D/(2·tan59°)). Keep boring-bar overhang within limits (page 16).

Worked Example

For a Ø10 H7 hole that must be precisely located: drill Ø9.7, bore to size and position, then ream for finish if needed. For a simpler hole already aligned: drill Ø9.7 then ream to Ø10 directly. The locked Ø10 point height is 3.004 mm when planning through depth.

Classroom assumption: drill size and stock are typical; confirm against the drill/reamer maker for the material.

Planning the depth correctly

For a Ø10 through hole in a 20 mm plate, the drill does not stop at 20 mm. The 118° point leaves a conical tip, so the total depth commanded is plate thickness plus the point height h = D/(2·tan59°) = 3.004 mm — about 23 mm. For the finishing sequence, leave a small radial stock (≈0.15 mm) after drilling at Ø9.7, then bore or ream. If the hole must be on position, bore before reaming; reaming follows an existing hole and will not correct a wandering one. Keep boring-bar overhang within limits (page 16), because a long, thin bar chatter even with the right tool. This case ties pages 17 and 18 together into one sequence rather than two isolated operations.

Inspection after the sequence

The plan is not done until the hole is checked. Measure diameter against the H7 tolerance, inspect the finish, and confirm the hole location against its datum on a CMM or with a bore gauge. If the diameter trends large across a batch, suspect reamer wear or reaming stock creeping up; if position is off, the boring step — not the ream — is where to look. Record the result on the wear-trial log so the sequence is repeatable. This closes the loop from tooling choice to inspection that the rest of module M8 expects.

When to bore and when to ream

The choice between the two finishing steps is decided by what the hole actually needs, not by preference. Reaming follows an existing, aligned hole and improves its size and surface; it cannot move the hole sideways or correct a wandering drill. So if the drilled hole is already on position and the only job left is to reach Ø10 H7 with a good finish, ream directly — cheaper and faster. If the position is wrong, or straightness matters and the drill walked, boring comes first: the boring bar cuts a true, located hole, and only then does the reamer size and finish it. A second check on stock: leave too little for the reamer and it rubs instead of cuts, giving a poor, oversized hole; leave too much and the reamer is overloaded and wears fast. The 0.15 mm radial figure is the classroom starting point, confirmed against the reamer maker for the material.

Why the reamer follows the drill, not the other way round

A reamer has several cutting edges guided by the hole it is already in — it is a sizing and finishing tool, not a locating tool. That is why it cannot rescue a mispositioned drill: it follows the existing bore and smooths it, reproducing its position and straightness rather than correcting them. The practical consequence is the ordering in the table: spot first to start true, drill near-net, then bore if position or straightness is off, and ream last to size and finish. It also explains why reaming stock must be small and even — if the drilled hole is out-of-round or tapered, the reamer's edges take an uneven bite and wander with the error instead of correcting it. This is the same logic as page 18: reaming assumes a good, aligned pre-hole, and the whole sequence exists to hand the reamer exactly that. When a reamed hole comes out off-location, the root cause is upstream in drilling or boring, never in the reamer itself.

Why spot drill comes before the twist drill

The first row of the sequence looks optional — a spot drill that only makes a shallow dimple — but it is what protects the position the rest of the steps depend on. A twist drill is self-centering only in an ideal world: its chisel point has poor centring action, and if it is fed straight onto a flat or slightly slanted surface it can wander or "walk" sideways before the full flutes engage, moving the hole off its programmed location. The spot drill (or spotface) cuts a flat, true starting cone exactly on the programmed XY, giving the drill's point a seat that cannot slip. This matters most on angled or curved surfaces and on holes that must be located to a datum, which is exactly the case here. It also improves tool life: a drill that starts true cuts symmetrically, instead of scrubbing in on one side and chipping its corner. The few seconds of the spot step is what makes the later bore-and-ream sequence worth planning — if the start position is wrong, no amount of finishing upstream of the reamer will put the hole back where the drawing wants it.

Why the inspection result points back to one step

The payoff of planning the sequence as distinct steps is that inspection failure localises the cause instantly. If the finished hole is the right diameter and finish but its position is off, the error entered at the boring (or spotting) step — the reamer cannot have moved it. If the position is true but the diameter trends large or the finish is rough, the problem is at the reaming step: reamer wear, too much stock, or wrong feed/coolant (page 18). If the hole is tapered or out-of-round, look at the drilled pre-hole and boring-bar rigidity (page 16), because an even reamer reproduces an uneven pre-hole. This is why the case separates spot, drill, bore and ream into its own table rather than treating them as one "make the hole" operation: each step owns one quality attribute, and the inspection result tells you which station to fix. A shop that runs drill-then-ream with no intermediate check has to guess which step caused a bad hole; a shop that inspects after each step can correct position at the bore and size at the ream without reworking the whole sequence.

Common Mistakes

  • Reaming a mislocated hole. Fix: bore first to correct position.
  • Forgetting point depth. Fix: add h = 3.004 mm for the Ø10 drill.

Practice

Q1. Drill for a Ø10 ream, leaving 0.15 mm radial stock? drill size?

Ø9.7 (10 − 2×0.15).

Q2. Hole off-center — ream or bore first?

Bore first to correct position, then ream if sizing/finish needed.
