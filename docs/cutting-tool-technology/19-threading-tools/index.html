Threading Tools

Threads are formed one light pass at a time, not in one plunge. Single-point threading, thread milling and tapping each use a different tool, and the infeed method decides whether the insert chips. This page covers pitch, multiple passes and infeed in plain terms.

Level: KPrerequisite: 16 Turning ToolsScope: metric; classroom examples
After this lesson, you can:
  • Choose single-point threading, thread milling or tapping for a job
  • Explain why threads need multiple light passes
  • Describe flank vs radial infeed and why it matters
  • Link threading to pitch and lead basics

Concept

A thread is a helical groove cut to a pitch. Three ways to make it: single-point threading (a V-shaped insert traces the helix on a lathe, over many passes), thread milling (a rotating thread-form mill interpolates around a hole), and tapping (a tap cuts internal threads in one tool). The key idea: the full thread depth is built up over several shallow passes, because the insert cannot take the whole thread depth in one plunge.

Why It Matters

Threads fail by chipping because the tool rubs and overheats near the end of each pass. Two design choices control this: how deep each pass cuts (decreasing pass depth so total chip load stays sane) and how the insert feeds in (radial infeed loads both flanks; flank infeed cuts one side at a time and runs cooler). Get the infeed wrong and the insert chips before the thread is finished.

How It Works

Single-point threading passes

The first passes cut most of the depth; each later pass cuts less. The insert must retract at the end of each pass before returning, to avoid dragging on the finished thread.

Infeed methods

Thread infeed methods
MethodCharacteristics
Radial infeedboth flanks cut, hotter, simpler; can chip on tough materials
Flank infeedone flank cuts, cooler, better chip flow, preferred for tough threads

Thread milling vs tapping

Tapping is fast for standard internal threads but a broken tap in a hole is costly. Thread milling is flexible for large or tough holes and avoids tap-break risk, but needs a milling machine with helical interpolation.

Worked Example

Cut an external M10×1.5 thread on a lathe. The pitch is 1.5 mm per turn, so the Z feed equals the pitch. The full thread depth is reached in several decreasing passes rather than one. Use flank infeed in steel so the chip flows and heat stays manageable; retract at the end of each pass. The same thread could be tapped if internal, or thread-milled on a machining center for a large blind hole.

Classroom assumption: pass counts and depths are general; the exact thread profile, depth and infeed come from the threading insert catalog and thread standard.

Threading at a glance

Threads are produced several ways: single-point turning on a lathe, taps for internal threads, and thread mills for larger or harder holes. Each cuts the same fundamental geometry — pitch, major/minor diameter, thread depth — but at very different productivity and setup cost. A tap is quick for standard holes but can snap if chip evacuation is poor; thread milling is flexible and avoids tap breakage in hard material; single-point threading is most controllable for odd or large forms. The critical machining detail is that feed equals the pitch per revolution, so the machine's feed and spindle are locked together. Whatever the method, start with the thread form and size from the drawing, then choose the tool that fits the machine and batch size rather than the other way around.

The tap-drill size you must set first

An internal thread is only as good as the minor (tap-drill) diameter you start with. Drill it too large and the tap has too little thread engagement; drill it too small and the tap is overloaded and snaps. For a metric thread, the common rule of thumb is tap drill diameter ≈ major diameter − pitch. For the M10×1.5 worked thread that gives roughly 10 − 1.5 = 8.5 mm pre-drill, which is why an 8.5 mm drill precedes an M10×1.5 tap. Get this backwards and no amount of speed or pecking saves the thread: a tap forced through an undersized hole breaks, and one through an oversized hole produces a thread that strips under load. This is the first decision in any tapping program, before spindle speed or peck cycles are chosen, and it links back to the drill and point-height planning on page 17 and the hole-quality ladder on page 18.

Why feed must equal pitch per revolution

In ordinary turning, feed per revolution sets how fast the tool advances relative to the cut; in threading it is locked to the thread pitch, not chosen. For an M10×1.5 thread the tool must advance exactly 1.5 mm for every one spindle turn, or the helix it traces does not match the pitch and the thread is ruined. This is why a lathe in threading mode synchronises the Z feed to spindle position rather than running an arbitrary feed rate, and why the spindle speed must be chosen carefully: too fast and the synchronised feed stretches or the tool cannot keep up, especially near the end of a long thread where acceleration and deceleration matter. It is also why a single-point threading pass must retract at the end before returning — on the return traverse the feed is no longer tied to the helix, and dragging the same path would scrape the finished flank. This pitch-locked feed is the fundamental difference between threading and any other turning operation, and it is why the thread program re-starts each pass in phase with the spindle rather than as a plain linear move.

Why a broken tap is feared, and how to avoid it

A tap failure is uniquely costly because the tool is inside the part when it snaps: a broken tap in a finished or near-finished hole often means scrapping the whole component, whereas a broken end mill can be pulled out and replaced. That asymmetry is why tapping has three design safeguards. First, the pre-drill (tap-drill) must be right — an undersized hole overloads the tap and is the most common breakage cause. Second, chip evacuation must work: in a blind hole, trapped chips bind the tap, so a spiral-flute or gun tap, or a peck cycle that retracts to clear chips, is required rather than a straight-flute tap run to the bottom. Third, tapping runs at much lower speed than drilling — the tap cuts a formed thread with weak chip flow and limited room to cool, so pushing spindle speed is a direct path to welding and snapping. This is exactly why thread milling exists for tough or large holes: it trades a rigid tapping operation with breakage risk for a milling cycle that cannot lock up inside the bore. When a tap breaks repeatedly, the diagnosis follows page 27 — pre-drill size first, then chip evacuation and speed, not a harder tap.

Why each later pass cuts less than the last

A threading pass is a V-shaped groove being widened incrementally, and the chip cross-section does not stay the same even if you took the same radial bite every time. If every pass plunged the same depth, the cutting edge would be engaged over a growing length of thread flank and the chip would swell, overloading the corner and heating the insert — which is why threading inserts chip toward the end of a long thread. The standard answer is decreasing infeed: the first passes take a meaningful bite, and each successive pass removes a smaller radial amount so the actual chip thickness stays roughly constant. This is the same logic as a chipbreaker window (page 06) applied to a groove: the edge wants a steady, manageable chip, not a growing one. It also means the number of passes is not arbitrary — the insert catalog prescribes the infeed schedule for the thread pitch and material, and a program that uses too few, too deep passes forces the edge to carry a chip it cannot break. When a threading insert wears fast or chips, check the infeed schedule before blaming speed; a too-coarse later pass is a common cause that the pass-count table exists to prevent.

Why threading runs at far lower RPM than turning

It surprises new operators that a threading pass uses a much lower spindle speed than the turning passes on the same part, and the reason is not that the edge needs more care — it is the pitch-locked feed. Because the Z axis must advance exactly one pitch per revolution, the feed rate (mm/min) is directly proportional to RPM: at 1000 RPM with a 1.5 mm pitch the tool travels at 1500 mm/min, and doubling the RPM doubles that synchronized feed. Two limits then cap the speed. First, the machine's feed and axis acceleration must keep up with a feed locked to the helix, especially at thread start and end where there is a short ramp before and after the cut; too fast and the first and last threads are out of phase. Second, the threading insert has a weak chip and limited clearance in the V-groove, so it generates heat quickly and wears if the surface speed climbs to normal turning values. The practical rule is to compute the threading RPM from a conservative surface speed for the thread insert, well below the turning speed of the same grade, and to leave a short overtravel at both ends so the helix is fully engaged before the cut reaches the shoulder and disengages before the tool decelerates. Low RPM here is a synchronization and heat constraint, not a sign that the operation is "slow work".

Common Mistakes

  • Taking the full depth in one pass. The insert overloads and chips. Fix: multiple decreasing passes.
  • Using radial infeed on a tough alloy. Overheats both flanks. Fix: flank infeed.
  • Forgetting to retract before returning. Drags on the finished thread. Fix: program the retract at each pass end.

Practice

Q1. Why not cut the whole thread depth at once?

The chip load would be too high; the insert would overheat and chip.

Q2. Infeed that cuts one flank at a time is called?

Flank infeed, cooler and better for tough materials.

Q3. Large blind thread in tough material — tap or thread mill?

Thread milling avoids the risk of a broken tap in a tough hole.
