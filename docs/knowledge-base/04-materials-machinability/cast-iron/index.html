04.3 · Materials & Machinability

Cast Iron

Cast iron is an alloy of iron with 2–4% carbon and 1–3% silicon. The carbon is not fully dissolved — it appears as graphite flakes or nodules in the microstructure. Castings are poured into molds near net shape, then CNC-machined to final dimensions. Cast iron machines differently from steel — it produces short chips, is abrasive, and doesn't need coolant.

Major Cast Iron Types

TypeMicrostructurePropertiesTypical use
Gray cast iron (GG20/GG25, ASTM A48)Flake graphiteExcellent vibration damping, good castability, brittleEngine blocks, machine bases, brake discs

Machining Cast Iron

What's easy

  • Short chips: gray cast iron fractures into small chips — no chip breaker needed, no tangling around the tool.
  • No coolant needed: cast iron machines dry. The graphite acts as a lubricant, and coolant causes the fine iron dust to form a sticky paste that clogs machines.
  • Good surface finish: cuts cleanly at moderate feeds.

What's hard

  • Abrasiveness: the graphite flakes and hard silicon particles cause flank wear on the tool. Use uncoated or TiCN-coated carbide (K-type grade).
  • Scale/skin: castings have a hard outer scale (the mold skin). Always take a cut deep enough to break through the scale on the first pass — cutting on the scale kills the tool edge.
  • Dust: cast iron generates fine, dark dust. Use a vacuum/extraction. The dust is conductive and can damage electrical components if it builds up in the machine.

Cast iron scale: the first cut on a casting must be deep enough (at least 0.5 mm) to get below the hard sand-blasted skin. Cutting on the scale causes rapid flank wear. If stock is uneven, take a facing pass first to find the real surface.
